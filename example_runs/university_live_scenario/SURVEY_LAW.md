# Faculty of Law — Initial Site & Feasibility Survey

**Stage:** university-survey (early feasibility)
**Site:** flat, level ground, approx. 60 m × 60 m (≈3,600 m²)
**Soil:** stratified (layered), generally suitable for standard shallow spread-footing foundations
**Brief:** lecture halls/classrooms for a mid-sized faculty, staff offices, cafeteria/canteen serving students and staff

---

## 1. Terrain and site conditions — approach

The plot is flat and level, which is a gift for layout and a liability for drainage. There is no natural fall to shed surface water, so the first design move is not architectural but hydrological.

**Approach:**

- **Set a finished floor level (FFL) above surrounding ground** — a raised plinth of 450–600 mm over the external ground level, with a graded apron falling away from the building on all sides. On a level site, the plinth is the cheapest and most reliable defence against ponding and against water tracking back into the building.
- **Design a positive drainage strategy before the footprint is fixed.** Because the site has no fall, stormwater must be collected and conveyed deliberately: perimeter slot/box drains, a graded paved forecourt, and connection to the campus storm system (or, if that is absent, infiltration via soakaway/attenuation crates sized to the local rainfall intensity). This is a site-wide decision that constrains where hardstanding can go.
- **Confirm the stratified soil profile with a small number of boreholes / trial pits** before committing to foundations. "Generally suitable for shallow spread footings" is a good starting position, but stratified ground means the *continuity* of the bearing layer matters more than its average strength. Two risks to check:
  1. A competent layer overlying a weaker or compressible layer, which can produce **differential settlement** where loads vary sharply across the plan (e.g. a tall, heavily loaded lecture-theatre block next to a light single-storey wing).
  2. **Perched water** sitting on a lower-permeability stratum, which would make shallow footings wet and would change the drainage design.
- **Foundation response:** strip footings under load-bearing walls and pad footings under columns, founded on the verified bearing stratum at a consistent depth. If the boreholes show a soft pocket or a layer boundary crossing under the concentrated lecture-theatre loads, use a **raft or a stiffened ground beam grid** under that block rather than deepening individual pads — it is more tolerant of local variation and cheaper than piling on a site that does not need it.
- **No slope stability, retaining, or dewatering issues are anticipated** on a level site with shallow foundations; the terrain risk here is water, not ground movement.

## 2. Zoning and footprint concept

Working assumption: a mid-sized law faculty of roughly **1,800–2,200 m² gross floor area**, delivered as a **2–3 storey building** rather than a sprawling single storey. On a 60 × 60 m plot this leaves genuine external space — a forecourt, a courtyard, and service access — instead of paving the whole site.

**Zoning, in plan:**

| Zone | Location | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Entrance forecourt / plaza | Street-facing edge | Arrival, identity, and the public face of the faculty; also the main hardstanding that must be graded to drain |
| Cafeteria / canteen pavilion | Street-facing corner, at ground level, with its own external door | It serves the whole campus, not just law students — it should be reachable without entering the teaching block, and it should be able to operate outside faculty hours |
| Teaching block (lecture halls, classrooms, seminar rooms) | Central / rear, stacked over 2–3 floors | The acoustic and structural core of the building; large column-free halls want to sit over each other with a common structural grid |
| Staff offices | Quiet side, upper floors, away from the cafeteria and from the main circulation | Needs daylight, acoustic separation from teaching and from kitchen noise/smell |
| Service yard (kitchen deliveries, refuse, plant) | Rear, screened, separate vehicle route | Kitchen deliveries and waste must not cross the student entrance; grease and refuse handling needs its own hardstanding and drainage |

**Key planning moves:**

- **Separate the pedestrian and service routes completely.** The kitchen's delivery door and the refuse store go at the rear; the cafeteria's customer entrance faces the plaza. This is a hygiene and operations decision as much as an architectural one.
- **Orient the teaching block for daylight without glare and for cross-ventilation** — deep-plan lecture halls are the exception, but classrooms and offices should be naturally lit and naturally ventilated where possible, which reduces plant and running cost.
- **Keep the cafeteria as a distinct volume** with its own roof and its own extract route. Kitchen exhaust, grease-laden and humid, should never be ducted through the office or teaching floors.
- **Phasing:** the teaching block and offices can be built first; the cafeteria pavilion is structurally and services-independent and can follow as a second phase without disrupting teaching.

## 3. Primary construction materials — general building

For the teaching and office accommodation I would use a **conventional reinforced-concrete frame with masonry infill**:

- **Structure:** in-situ reinforced concrete columns, beams and slabs on a regular grid (typically 7.5–8.5 m). RC is the right default here: it is robust, fire-resistant without added protection, thermally massive (useful for a hot climate and for evening-out daily temperature swings), locally available, and well understood by local trades. It also handles the wet, hard-wearing ground floors of a public building well.
- **External walls:** clay brick or concrete block infill, rendered and painted, with insulated cavity or external insulation where the climate requires it. Masonry gives good acoustic separation between teaching rooms and is durable and repairable.
- **Windows / façade:** aluminium-framed glazing with solar-control glass and external shading (brise-soleil, deep reveals, or screens) on the exposed elevations. Shading is cheaper than cooling.
- **Internal partitions:** blockwork or stud-and-board for offices and seminar rooms; blockwork where acoustic separation matters (between lecture halls and circulation).
- **Roof:** reinforced concrete slab with a waterproof membrane, insulation and a protective screed, laid to falls. On a flat site with no natural drainage, roof falls and outlets must be generous and well-maintained — ponding is the most common long-term failure of flat roofs.
- **Floors:** terrazzo, porcelain tile or polished concrete in public and circulation areas; carpet or resilient sheet in offices; hard-wearing tile in teaching rooms.
- **Ceilings:** exposed soffit in circulation and teaching (thermal mass, fewer materials), suspended acoustic ceilings only where acoustic control genuinely requires it.

**Why not a lightweight steel or timber frame as the default?** Steel is efficient for long spans (see the cafeteria below) but needs fire protection and is less thermally massive; timber is attractive for carbon but introduces moisture and acoustic detailing risk in a building with kitchens and heavy public use. RC is the low-risk, low-maintenance backbone for the teaching and office accommodation. Steel is reserved for where it earns its place — the long-span halls.

## 4. Cafeteria / canteen — materials and detailing

The cafeteria is the most demanding part of the brief and should be designed as a **commercial kitchen with a dining room attached**, not as a dining room with a kitchenette. Structural span, fire rating, hygiene, moisture resistance and maintenance all pull in the same direction: hard, non-porous, cleanable, fire-separated, and replaceable.

**Structural span.**
- The dining area wants to be **column-free over roughly 12–15 m** so tables can be laid out flexibly and the room can be reconfigured for events. A reinforced-concrete flat slab will not do this economically.
- Use a **steel portal frame or steel roof trusses** over the dining hall, or **post-tensioned concrete beams** if a concrete soffit is preferred for thermal mass and finish. Steel is the simpler answer for a single-storey pavilion and gives a clean, light roof structure.
- The **kitchen itself does not need long spans** — it is a cellular, heavily serviced space. Keep it on a short grid with a concrete slab, because it carries heavy equipment loads, floor drainage, and the fire separation.

**Fire rating.**
- The kitchen is a **high fire-load space** (cooking oils, gas, packaging, refuse). It must be separated from the dining room and from the rest of the building by a **fire-rated construction — typically 60 minutes, and 120 minutes where the kitchen abuts the teaching block or a means of escape**.
- **Fire-rated doors and self-closing shutters** between kitchen and dining; fire dampers where ducts cross the separation.
- The **kitchen extract duct must be a fire-rated, grease-tight enclosure** with access for cleaning, and the cooking line should sit under an **automatic suppression system** (wet chemical over the range, plus a hood suppression). This is a life-safety requirement, not an optional extra.
- Structural steel in the dining hall needs **intumescent coating or board protection** to achieve its fire rating — factor this into the material choice and the maintenance regime.

**Hygiene.**
- **Floors:** slip-resistant, seamless, coved to the walls — epoxy resin or heavy-duty quarry/porcelain tile with a coved skirting. No joints where dirt and water can collect. Falls to a trapped floor drain in the kitchen.
- **Walls:** smooth, impervious, washable. Stainless-steel or FRP panel systems behind cooking and washing lines; glazed tile or epoxy-coated blockwork elsewhere. **No gypsum board, no painted plaster, no timber** in the kitchen wet zones.
- **Ceilings:** cleanable metal panel or sealed, washable system in the kitchen — **not** mineral-fibre acoustic tile, which absorbs grease and moisture and cannot be cleaned.
- **Work surfaces and equipment:** stainless steel throughout the food-preparation areas; stainless or heavy-duty laminate in the dining servery.
- **Dining room:** hard, cleanable finishes — porcelain tile or terrazzo floor, wipeable walls, easy-clean tables. Comfortable, but nothing that cannot be washed.

**Moisture resistance.**
- The kitchen generates **steam, grease-laden vapour and frequent washdown**. The response is a **dedicated, generously sized extract system** with make-up air, a vapour-tight construction, and a **waterproof membrane under the kitchen floor** with a drained, coved finish.
- **Avoid moisture-sensitive materials entirely in the kitchen envelope** — no plasterboard, no untreated timber, no absorbent insulation exposed to the interior.
- The **roof over the kitchen** should be detailed with a robust waterproof membrane and generous falls; a ventilated metal roof over the kitchen zone is a reasonable alternative that sheds grease-laden condensate more predictably than a flat membrane roof.
- Dining room glazing should be detailed for **high internal humidity and condensation** — thermally broken frames, and ventilation that does not rely on opening windows next to the servery.

**Maintenance.**
- Everything in the kitchen should be **replaceable without demolition**: panel systems, removable ceiling tiles (metal), accessible ducts, and a floor finish that can be patched.
- **Stainless steel and epoxy/tile are chosen precisely because they survive decades of daily washdown**; painted plaster and gypsum board do not, and would need repainting or replacement within a few years.
- **Grease duct cleaning access** and **floor drain rodding access** must be designed in from the start — retrofitting them is expensive and is a common cause of kitchen fires and closures.
- The dining hall's steel structure needs a **maintenance-painted or galvanised** finish, with intumescent protection inspected periodically.

**Summary of cafeteria material choices:**

| Element | Choice | Driver |
|---|---|---|
| Dining hall structure | Steel portal frame / trusses (or PT concrete) | 12–15 m clear span |
| Kitchen structure | RC slab on short grid | Equipment loads, drainage, fire separation |
| Fire separation | 60–120 min rated walls, rated doors, fire-rated grease duct, suppression | Life safety, high fire load |
| Kitchen floor | Epoxy or quarry tile, coved, drained | Hygiene, moisture, cleanability |
| Kitchen walls | Stainless / FRP panels, glazed tile | Hygiene, washdown |
| Kitchen ceiling | Metal panel, sealed | Cleanable, grease-resistant |
| Dining floor | Porcelain tile / terrazzo | Durability, cleanability |
| Roof over kitchen | Membrane with falls, or ventilated metal | Moisture, grease condensate |
| Steel protection | Intumescent coating / boards, galvanised | Fire rating, maintenance |

## 5. Influence of peer findings (open_questions)

Four peer findings were live in `open_questions` at the time this survey was finalised. I assessed each independently against my own site and brief:

1. **[Computing] Grease-laden kitchen exhaust duct as a fire hazard** — dedicated liquid-tight grease duct, access doors at each change of direction, fire-rated enclosure where it passes other occupancies, termination clear of intakes, dedicated hood+duct suppression interlocked to shut down the fan and fuel, grease trap on drainage, fire-rated separation of kitchen from dining and teaching. **Accepted and applied.** My survey already treated the kitchen as a high fire-load compartment with a fire-rated grease-tight duct and wet-chemical suppression; this finding sharpened the detailing (access doors at every change of direction, duct termination clear of air intakes and windows, interlock to the supply fan and fuel, and a grease interceptor on the drainage line). It also confirmed the early-planning point that the duct drives ceiling-void depth, roof penetrations and shaft location — which is why the cafeteria is zoned as a distinct volume with its own extract route rather than having its duct run through the teaching floors.

2. **[Business] Lightweight drywall/mineral-fibre ceilings unsuitable in kitchen and dishwash zones; kitchen as a 60-min compartment; coved, tanked, drained wet-area floors** — **Accepted and applied.** This is the same conclusion I reached independently in §4 (no gypsum board, no mineral-fibre ceiling in the kitchen; non-combustible, non-porous, washable surfaces; coved slip-resistant floors with falls to drains). The finding reinforced the decision to keep the kitchen's floor build-up and compartmentation entirely separate from the dry teaching spaces, and to treat the kitchen as a 60-minute (120-minute where it abuts the teaching block) compartment.

3. **[Engineering] Gypsum plasterboard and timber-based linings unsuitable for kitchen/wash-down zones; cementitious board, masonry or tiled concrete; resin/epoxy or safety-vinyl floor screeds with coved skirting and trapped channel drainage; washable metal/vinyl-faced ceilings; 60–120 min non-combustible enclosure, easier in masonry/concrete than lightweight drywall** — **Accepted and applied.** This is consistent with my §4 material schedule. It reinforced the choice of masonry/concrete for the kitchen's fire separation (which is why I keep the kitchen on a concrete slab and short grid rather than a lightweight frame) and the trapped channel drainage detail.

4. **[Arts] Geotechnical caution on stratified soil — bearing capacity is not uniformity; verify stratum continuity across the whole footprint, keep column bases on the same stratum or step/deepen footings, consider a stiffened strip or raft where a large-span or heavily loaded area crosses a transition, and design slabs/drainage to tolerate small differential movement** — **Accepted and applied.** This directly reinforced and sharpened §1. My original approach already called for boreholes and for a raft or stiffened ground-beam grid under the concentrated lecture-theatre loads; the finding added the specific actions of verifying stratum continuity with a *grid* of boreholes/trial pits across the whole footprint (not one central hole), keeping column bases on a single stratum or stepping/deepening across a transition, and detailing movement joints and flexible service connections so the ground-floor slabs and drainage tolerate small differential movement. Because my teaching block stacks large column-free halls over a common grid, this is a real risk on my site and the finding is material to it.

**Net effect:** none of the four findings changed the fundamental concept — the RC-frame-plus-masonry teaching block, the distinct cafeteria pavilion, and the plinth-and-positive-drainage response to the level site all stand. All four were consistent with the direction I had already taken, and each sharpened a specific detail (grease-duct access and interlock; kitchen compartmentation and wet-area floors; masonry/concrete fire separation; borehole grid and movement-tolerant foundations). No finding was rejected as inapplicable to this site.
