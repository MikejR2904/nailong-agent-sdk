# Faculty of Computing — Building Plan (Final)

**Stage:** university-plan (post-survey, all six faculty surveys read)
**Site:** flat, level ground, ~60 m × 60 m (≈3,600 m² gross plot)
**Soil:** stratified (layered), generally suitable for standard shallow spread-footing foundations
**Brief:** lecture halls/classrooms for a mid-sized faculty, staff offices, cafeteria/canteen serving students and staff.

This plan finalises the footprint/zoning and the material choices, especially for the
cafeteria, that were left open in `SURVEY_COMPUTING.md`. It is written after reading all
six faculty surveys — my own and my five peers' (Engineering, Business, Law, Medicine,
Arts). Section 6 states explicitly what, if anything, a peer's survey changed.

---

## 1. Site strategy (carried forward, with peer-driven refinements)

The site facts are unchanged from my survey: flat, level ground; stratified soil suitable
for shallow spread footings; no slope, no retaining, no cut-and-fill problem. The design
risk on this site is therefore **water and differential settlement**, not earthworks.

**Foundations — final decision.** Standard shallow reinforced-concrete pad footings under
columns and strip footings under load-bearing walls, founded on the verified bearing
stratum, tied together by a **reinforced ground-beam / tie-beam grid**. No piling is
anticipated; a raft is held in reserve only for a bay that cannot avoid a stratum
transition.

**Ground investigation — final decision.** A **borehole / trial-pit grid across the whole
footprint** (not one central hole), including a hole at the cafeteria corner, to establish
stratum continuity, depth and thickness of each layer, the selected bearing stratum and its
allowable bearing pressure, and the presence of any soft, shrinkable or organic layer.
This is the single most valuable pre-design action on this site.

**Perched water — added check.** Layered soils commonly hold water on top of a
lower-permeability stratum. I will require the ground investigation to report specifically
on a **perched water table**, not just the regional water table, because it drives
foundation depth, damp-proofing and the drainage design. (See §6 — this check was added in
response to peer surveys.)

**Drainage — final decision.** Flat ground means drainage is designed, not assumed:
- Finished floor level set **450–600 mm above surrounding external ground**, with a
  perimeter apron and a positive fall (min. 1:60) away from the building on all sides.
- Perimeter channel/slot drains plus a positive piped storm system to the site outfall.
  The **outfall invert depth governs the design** and must be confirmed before the
  footprint is frozen; if it is shallow, attenuation (below-ground crates) or a short
  pumped solution is required for the cafeteria wet areas.
- Roof falls and generous outlets; ponding is the default failure mode of a flat roof on a
  flat site.
- All external circulation step-free (max 1:20 ramps where levels change).

**Orientation.** Long teaching elevations oriented to minimise low-angle east/west solar
gain; cafeteria glazing placed for daylight without direct afternoon sun on the dining
area. Deep overhangs / external shading on sun-facing elevations — relevant because
computing teaching spaces have high internal heat gains from equipment and occupancy.

## 2. Footprint and zoning (final)

**Concept: a compact two-storey (part three-storey) teaching block with a clearly
separated, single-storey cafeteria wing, arranged around a small arrival courtyard.**

Indicative footprint on the 3,600 m² plot:

| Zone | Indicative footprint | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Main teaching block | ~45 m × 30 m ≈ **1,350 m²** (two storeys → ~2,700 m² gross) | Lecture halls, classrooms, seminar rooms, stacked vertically |
| Staff/administration | within main block | Offices, meeting rooms, staff facilities |
| Cafeteria/canteen wing | ~25 m × 18 m ≈ **450 m²** | Dining hall + kitchen + servery + stores + waste |
| Circulation / atrium / stairs | within blocks | Vertical and horizontal circulation, toilets |
| External | remainder (~1,800 m²) | Arrival plaza, courtyard, service yard, planting, drainage |

Total built area in the order of **3,100–3,200 m² gross** — a reasonable mid-sized faculty,
and consistent with the order of magnitude my peers reached independently.

**Zoning logic:**
- **Teaching block** on the quieter, main-approach side. Large-span lecture halls at ground
  and first floor where column-free volume is easiest; smaller classrooms and offices
  above/beside. Vertical stacking keeps the footprint compact on a modest plot and shortens
  service runs; a single-storey scheme would consume most of the plot and leave no room for
  the service yard and drainage.
- **Cafeteria wing** on a separate edge, with its own service yard, delivery access and a
  dedicated kitchen extract route to roof. It is deliberately **structurally and
  fire-separated** from the teaching block — the cafeteria is the highest-hazard,
  highest-servicing element of the brief and must not be buried in the middle of the plan.
- **Kitchen** at the back of the cafeteria wing against the service yard; dining hall at the
  front with daylight; servery between them. This keeps the grease-producing zone away from
  teaching spaces and gives a short, direct extract duct run to a roof discharge.
- **Service separation:** deliveries, waste and grease collection use the service yard and
  never cross the main student circulation.
- **Structural grid:** a regular bay (~7.5–8.5 m) so classrooms, offices and the cafeteria
  dining hall all land on the same grid, with lecture halls spanning two bays where a clear
  span is needed. A repetitive grid is exactly what the layered-soil condition rewards.

## 3. Primary construction materials — building generally (final)

- **Structure:** reinforced-concrete frame (columns, beams, flat or beam-and-block slabs) on
  shallow pad/strip footings with a tied ground-beam grid. Rationale: suits shallow
  foundations; robust and low-maintenance; inherent fire resistance without applied
  protection; good acoustic mass between stacked teaching floors; and a stiff, monolithic
  frame tolerates and redistributes the small differential movements that a layered profile
  can produce. Long-span lecture halls use deeper concrete beams or a steel roof structure
  over the concrete frame.
- **Upper floors / roofs:** concrete slabs for teaching floors (acoustic separation,
  vibration control for equipment); lightweight steel or timber roof structure over the top
  floor with a warm flat roof (insulated, membrane-covered). Flat roofs suit the square plot
  and give clean roof space for the kitchen extract discharge and plant.
- **External walls:** insulated masonry cavity wall (blockwork inner leaf, brick or rendered
  block outer leaf) as the baseline — durable, low-maintenance, good acoustic and thermal
  mass, familiar local construction. Rainscreen or lightweight cladding panels acceptable on
  feature elevations and around the cafeteria glazing.
- **Glazing:** aluminium-framed double-glazed units with solar-control glass on sun-facing
  elevations; openable sections for natural ventilation in classrooms, with mechanical
  ventilation/comfort cooling where equipment loads demand it.
- **Internal partitions (dry areas only):** blockwork or stud-and-board partitions with
  acoustic insulation between teaching rooms; fire-rated boards where compartment lines
  require it. **These dry partitions are not carried into the kitchen or wash-down zones**
  (see §4).
- **Finishes (dry areas):** hard-wearing, low-maintenance — sealed concrete or vinyl/ceramic
  floors in circulation and teaching, painted plasterboard/blockwork walls, suspended
  ceilings in offices and corridors to conceal services.

## 4. Cafeteria/canteen — finalised materials and construction

The cafeteria is the element with the most demanding combination of requirements:
**structural span** (column-free dining), **fire rating** (cooking hazard), **hygiene and
moisture resistance** (food preparation, washing, steam, grease) and **maintenance** (heavy
use, cleaning chemicals, grease). It is specified separately from the teaching palette.

### 4.1 Structural span
- **Dining hall:** long-span, near-column-free roof over the dining area — either a deep
  reinforced-concrete beam/ribbed slab or, more economically for a clear span, a steel roof
  structure (portal or truss) with metal deck and insulated membrane roof. Target clear span
  ~12–15 m.
- **Kitchen and servery:** conventional column grid; the kitchen is a cellular, heavily
  serviced space and does not need long spans. Kitchen floor is a **level, tanked, structural
  slab** — no steps, no timber, no lightweight raised floor.

### 4.2 Fire rating and compartmentation
- The kitchen is a **high fire-hazard compartment**, separated from the dining hall and from
  all adjacent teaching/staff space by **fire-rated, non-combustible construction — 60
  minutes minimum, designed to 120 minutes** where it abuts the teaching block or a means of
  escape — with self-closing fire doors and fire dampers where ducts cross the separation.
- Fire-rated ceiling/roof construction over the kitchen and over the extract route.
- **Kitchen grease-laden exhaust** is the specific hazard: grease vapour deposits on duct
  walls and can ignite from a cookline flare-up, and fire can travel *inside* the duct
  through otherwise fire-separated compartments. Therefore:
  - a dedicated, continuously welded or flanged **liquid-tight grease duct** (not ordinary
    galvanised spiral duct), with access doors at every change of direction for cleaning;
  - the duct enclosed in a **fire-rated shaft** or wrapped with a listed fire-rated system
    where it passes through or adjacent to other occupancies, terminating well clear of air
    intakes, windows and combustible roof build-up;
  - a dedicated **wet-chemical kitchen suppression system (hood + duct)**, interlocked to
    shut down the supply fan and fuel supply on activation;
  - a **grease trap/interceptor** on the kitchen drainage line.
- This drives ceiling void depth, roof penetrations and shaft locations, so it is fixed early
  in the zoning layout (§2) rather than retrofitted.

### 4.3 Hygiene and moisture resistance (final material schedule)
- **Kitchen walls:** cementitious / glass-mat board, masonry, or tiled concrete — non-
  combustible, non-porous, washable. Stainless-steel or FRP wall panels in the heaviest
  wash-down zones; ceramic tiling to splash and working heights elsewhere. Sealed, easily
  cleaned junctions.
- **Kitchen ceilings:** washable metal or vinyl-faced panels, or a sealed, painted concrete
  soffit. **Not mineral-fibre tiles** — they absorb grease and moisture, sag and stain, and
  cannot be cleaned.
- **Kitchen floors:** slip-resistant epoxy-resin or safety-vinyl screed (or quarry/ceramic
  tile) with **coved skirting** to walls, laid to falls to **trapped floor drains**, and
  **tanked** to prevent water reaching the structure.
- **Explicit exclusions in the kitchen and all wash-down zones:** **no paper-faced gypsum
  board, no painted plaster, no timber-based linings, no absorbent insulation exposed to the
  interior, and no mineral-fibre ceilings.** These degrade under repeated wet cleaning and
  grease-laden airborne moisture, harbour mould in the paper facing, and cannot deliver the
  required hygiene, moisture and fire performance.
- **Stainless-steel** worktops, sinks, splashbacks and equipment stands; stainless or
  coated-metal ductwork and hoods.
- **Dining hall:** hard-wearing, easily mopped floor (vinyl, tile or sealed concrete) with
  coved skirting, durable wipe-clean wall finishes to at least splash height, and a
  moisture-tolerant, cleanable ceiling — less stringent than the kitchen but still specified
  for spillage and cleaning. Acoustic treatment matters: a busy dining hall of hard surfaces
  alone is unusable for conversation.

### 4.4 Maintenance
All cafeteria finishes chosen for cleanability and repairability: replaceable floor tiles,
accessible duct cleaning doors at every change of direction, removable ceiling panels for
service access outside the wet zones, and durable, scrubbable wall finishes. Kitchen extract
and grease interceptor require a scheduled cleaning regime — a maintenance commitment, not
just a design item, and recorded in the facilities brief.

### 4.5 Services
Kitchen needs high-capacity mechanical extract with make-up air, generous cold and hot water
supply, gas or electric cooking supply, and a drainage line sized for grease-laden waste with
an interceptor. Dining hall needs good ventilation and daylight. All of this concentrates
services in one wing — another reason to zone the cafeteria as a distinct, service-rich block.

## 5. Risks and open items for the next stage

- Confirm stratified-soil layer depths, bearing capacity and **perched water** by ground
  investigation before fixing foundation design; the layered profile is the main geotechnical
  uncertainty.
- Confirm the **outfall invert depth** and capacity before freezing the footprint; it governs
  whether the cafeteria wet areas drain by gravity or need attenuation/pumping.
- Confirm local fire code compartment sizes and required fire resistance periods for the
  kitchen and its extract duct.
- Confirm kitchen fuel type (gas vs electric) — affects services, ventilation and suppression
  design.
- Confirm planning/height limits, which will cap the vertical stacking strategy.
- Confirm incoming water main size/pressure and electrical supply capacity (cafeteria-driven).

## 6. Influence of peer findings — what changed and what did not

I read all five peer surveys. My own survey (`SURVEY_COMPUTING.md`) recorded that no peer
finding had arrived before it was written, so this is the first point at which I can assess
them. I assessed each independently against my own flat, stratified site and my own brief.

### 6.1 Findings that DID change or firm up a decision

**(a) Faculty of Arts — stratified soil: bearing capacity ≠ uniformity.**
Arts' survey makes the point that "suitable for shallow spread footings" is a statement
about bearing capacity, not uniformity: on a layered profile a single large footprint can
straddle a stratum transition and settle differentially even though every individual footing
passes its own check. **This changed my plan.** My survey already called for a borehole
investigation and a tied ground-beam grid, but it treated the investigation loosely ("3–4
boreholes"). I have now firmed it up to a **grid across the whole footprint, including the
cafeteria corner**, and I have added two things I had not specified: **movement joints and
flexible service connections** so the ground-floor slabs and drainage tolerate small
differential movement, and a **raft held in reserve** for any bay that cannot avoid a
transition. This is directly material to my site because my teaching block stacks large
column-free halls over a common grid, and the cafeteria wing is a separate, heavier,
service-dense block — exactly the load pattern that exposes a stratum transition.

**(b) Faculty of Business and Faculty of Engineering — wet-zone material warnings.**
Both peers independently found that **paper-faced gypsum board, timber-based linings and
mineral-fibre suspended ceilings are unsuitable in kitchen and dishwash zones**, and both
specify non-combustible, non-porous, washable surfaces (cement/glass-mat board, stainless or
FRP panels, tiled concrete), **coved, tanked, slip-resistant floors with falls to trapped
drains**, and washable metal/vinyl-faced ceilings. **This changed my plan.** My survey had
specified only "moisture-resistant, wipe-clean panels" and "ceramic tiling or smooth,
washable panel/board" — directionally right but not explicit, and it did not name the
materials to exclude. I have now written an explicit exclusion list into §4.3 (no gypsum, no
painted plaster, no timber, no mineral-fibre ceilings in the kitchen or wash-down zones) and
named the accepted materials (cementitious/glass-mat board, stainless/FRP, tiled concrete,
metal or vinyl-faced ceilings, tanked coved floors). Engineering's additional point — that a
60–120 min non-combustible kitchen enclosure is easier to achieve in masonry/concrete than in
lightweight drywall — is consistent with my RC-frame-plus-masonry decision and reinforced it.

**(c) Faculty of Medicine and Faculty of Law — perched water on layered soil.**
Both peers flag that layered soils commonly hold water on top of a lower-permeability
stratum, and that this **perched water table** matters more than the regional water table for
foundation depth, damp-proofing and drainage. **This changed my plan.** My survey had said
only "no evidence of a high water table is given." I have now added an explicit requirement
that the ground investigation report on perched water, and I have linked it to foundation
depth and the drainage design (§1, §5).

### 6.2 Findings that did NOT change anything

- **Faculty of Engineering's hillside-specific findings** — terracing, retaining walls,
  cut-and-fill balance, slope-stability analysis, cut-off drains above the building, and
  traversing access roads — **do not apply to my site.** My plot is flat and level; there is
  no slope, no retaining and no cut-and-fill question. I have not imported any of these into
  my plan, and I would be fabricating a connection if I claimed otherwise. Engineering's
  *cafeteria* findings (grease duct, wet-zone materials) do apply and are covered in (b)
  above; only its *terrain* findings are inapplicable.
- **Faculty of Law's and Faculty of Medicine's surveys** otherwise reach the same conclusions
  I had already reached independently (RC frame on shallow footings, distinct cafeteria wing,
  plinth-and-positive-drainage response to a level site, kitchen as a fire compartment). They
  confirmed my direction but required no change beyond the perched-water point in (c).
- **My own finding** (`finding-computing-1647d8c9`, the grease-laden kitchen exhaust hazard)
  was adopted by Engineering, Business, Law, Medicine and Arts. That is my finding being
  taken up by peers, not a peer finding changing my plan — so it is not counted here as a
  peer-driven change. It remains the basis of my §4.2.

### 6.3 Net effect

Three peer-driven changes to my plan: (a) the stratified-soil foundation approach was firmed
up to a whole-footprint borehole grid with movement joints, flexible connections and a raft
reserve (Arts); (b) the cafeteria wet-zone material schedule was made explicit, with named
exclusions and named accepted materials (Business + Engineering); (c) a perched-water-table
check was added to the ground investigation (Medicine + Law). No peer finding was rejected as
inapplicable to my site except Engineering's terrain-specific findings, which genuinely do
not apply to a flat plot. The fundamental concept — RC-frame teaching block with a distinct,
fire-separated cafeteria wing on a plinth with designed positive drainage — is unchanged and
was independently confirmed by every peer.
