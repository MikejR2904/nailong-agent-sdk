# Faculty of Engineering — Building Plan

**Stage:** university-plan (post-survey, all six faculty surveys read)
**Site:** hillside plot, ~80 m across-slope × 100 m up-slope; ~18 m fall uphill-to-downhill (~18% average grade, locally steeper)
**Brief:** lecture halls/classrooms for a mid-sized faculty, staff offices, cafeteria/canteen for students and staff.
**Basis:** my own survey (SURVEY_ENGINEERING.md) plus the five peer surveys (Computing, Business, Law, Medicine, Arts), all read in full before this plan was finalised.

---

## 1. Governing site facts (unchanged from survey)

The 18 m cross-fall is the dominant design fact. The building absorbs the level change internally; the site is not flattened. Cut-and-fill balance and slope stability are the governing engineering issues, and they precede layout. Ground investigation and slope-stability analysis are commissioned before the foundation design is fixed.

---

## 2. Footprint and zoning (finalised)

**Concept: a stepped spine with terraced wings.**

- **Spine block** runs *across* the slope, along the contour, at mid-height. This is the most efficient orientation — a building running along the contour needs the least cut/fill for a given length, and its longest dimension has the gentlest ground relationship. The main entrance sits in the spine at the level where the access road arrives.
- **Upper terrace (uphill, quieter):** lecture halls and classrooms. Large-span, low-service, acoustically sensitive; kept away from the service road and the kitchen, on the more stable upper ground.
- **Mid spine:** staff offices, seminar rooms, circulation, main entrance. Offices are the most forgiving of a stepped floor plate and the easiest to subdivide.
- **Lower terrace (downhill, serviced):** cafeteria/canteen, kitchen, and the service yard. The kitchen wants a level floor, a service road and a drainage outfall — all easier at the bottom of the slope. It also keeps the kitchen's fire and odour risk at the downhill edge, away from teaching spaces and away from the up-slope air intakes.
- **Footprint discipline:** compact, rectangular blocks rather than one long continuous slab, so each block sits on a consistent stratum and each terrace edge is a short, well-defined retaining line. No single column-free mega-footprint straddling the slope.
- **Circulation:** a vertical core (stairs + lift) at the spine; the accessible route between terraces is the lift plus a ramped route at a compliant gradient. Fire escape from the lower terrace leads *across* the slope to a place of safety, not up it.
- **External works:** terraced external spaces — a level entrance plaza and a level dining terrace outside the cafeteria — created without flattening the site.
- **Retaining:** structural retaining walls at every terrace edge and along the uphill cut face, designed for at-rest or higher earth pressures, with filter drainage and weep outlets, founded below the zone of seasonal moisture movement; soil-nailed or anchored solutions where walls are tall.
- **Drainage:** a cut-off drain *above* the building intercepts up-slope water and carries it around; terrace water is collected and discharged to a controlled outfall, never sheet-flowing down the slope face.

## 3. Structural system and general materials (finalised)

- **Structure:** reinforced concrete frame (in-situ or precast) as the default. It handles the stepped levels and cantilevers cleanly, is inherently fire-resistant without added protection, tolerates the movement and moisture of a sloping site, and gives the mass and stiffness that helps acoustic separation between lecture halls. **Steel is used selectively for the large-span roofs over lecture halls**, with fire protection and careful detailing at the stepped interfaces.
- **Floors:** concrete slabs throughout; no suspended timber floors in teaching or circulation areas.
- **External envelope:** masonry or concrete with a drained, ventilated rainscreen or a rendered/insulated concrete finish, detailed for driving rain and for the splash zone at terrace level. No large areas of lightweight, many-jointed cladding on the exposed uphill face.
- **Roof:** concrete or metal standing-seam roof with robust falls and generous gutters/outlets, discharging into the terrace drainage system — never directly onto the slope face. **Exception over the kitchen: a ventilated metal roof** (see §4 and §5).
- **Internal partitions (dry areas):** robust-grade, moisture-resistant drywall in offices and dry teaching spaces only; never in wet or grease zones.

## 4. Cafeteria / kitchen — finalised material choices

The cafeteria is the most demanding element of the brief and the one where material choice is least forgiving.

**Structure and span**
- Kitchen and dining are single-storey-height spaces within the lower terrace block. Dining needs a clear span of roughly 12–15 m; a **reinforced concrete beam-and-slab or a steel beam with concrete slab** achieves this economically. The kitchen is smaller and cellular and is conventionally framed.
- The kitchen floor is a **level, tanked, structural slab** — no steps, no timber, no lightweight raised floor.

**Fire rating and compartmentation**
- The kitchen is a **high fire-hazard compartment**, separated from the dining area and from all teaching/office spaces by a **fire-rated, non-combustible wall (60 minutes minimum; designed to 120 minutes given the grease load and the proximity of the dining space)**, with self-closing fire doors. **Fire dampers are provided where ducts cross the compartment line.**
- **Grease-laden exhaust duct:** a dedicated, continuously welded or flanged **liquid-tight grease duct** — *not* ordinary galvanised spiral duct — with access doors at every change of direction for cleaning. It is enclosed in a fire-rated shaft or wrapped with a listed fire-rated system wherever it passes through or adjacent to other occupancies, and terminated well clear of air intakes, windows and combustible roof build-up.
- **Kitchen suppression:** wet-chemical hood and duct suppression, interlocked to shut down the supply fan and fuel on activation. A grease trap/interceptor on the drainage line.

**Hygiene and moisture resistance**
- **No gypsum plasterboard and no timber-based linings anywhere in the kitchen or wash-down zones.**
- **Kitchen walls:** cementitious board, masonry, or tiled concrete — non-combustible, non-porous, washable. Stainless-steel or FRP wall panels in the heaviest wash-down zones.
- **Kitchen ceilings:** washable metal or vinyl-faced panels, or a sealed, painted concrete soffit — not mineral-fibre tiles.
- **Floors:** resin/epoxy or safety-vinyl screed with coved skirting, laid to falls to trapped channel drainage; slip-resistant; tanked to prevent water reaching the structure.
- **Roof over the kitchen:** a **ventilated metal roof** rather than a flat membrane roof, so grease-laden condensate sheds predictably (see §5).
- **Dining area:** a more forgiving palette, but still a hard, easily-cleaned floor (vinyl or tile) with coved skirting and washable wall finishes to at least splash height. **Acoustic treatment is added to the dining hall** — hard surfaces alone make a busy dining hall unusable for conversation (see §5).

**Maintenance**
- Every choice above is driven by maintenance as much as first cost: stainless, tile, resin and metal-panel finishes are cleanable, repairable and long-lived; a kitchen that cannot be hosed down, or whose ceiling must be replaced every few years, is a false economy.

## 5. Influence of the peer surveys — what changed and what did not

My own survey had already adopted three findings that were live during the survey stage: **Computing** (grease-laden kitchen exhaust), **Business** (wet-zone finishes/compartmentation) and **Arts** (stratified-soil differential settlement). Those remain adopted and are carried into §§2–4 unchanged. Reading the five peer surveys in full now, I found a further set of items. I have separated genuine changes from confirmations, and I have not invented connections that are not there.

### 5a. Peer items that CHANGED a decision I would otherwise have made

1. **Law and Medicine — perched water table on a lower-permeability stratum.** My survey's ground-investigation scope listed groundwater level and its seasonal variation, but not *perched* water specifically. Law and Medicine both flag that layered soils commonly hold water on top of a less permeable stratum, and that this — not the regional water table — drives foundation depth and drainage design. **Change:** the ground investigation now explicitly includes a perched-water check, and the retaining-wall design now assumes perched water pressure behind the wall (filter drain + weep outlets sized for it, wall founded below the zone of seasonal moisture movement). On a hillside with retaining walls at every terrace edge this is a real design driver, not a formality.

2. **Arts — aggressive ground chemistry and made ground.** Arts adds two feasibility checks my survey did not name: sulphate/aggressive ground chemistry (affects concrete mix and buried services) and made ground or buried obstructions from previous use. **Change:** the concrete specification for foundations and buried retaining structures now includes a sulphate/aggressive-ground check, with a sulphate-resisting mix and increased cover where required, and the ground investigation includes a made-ground/obstruction check. This is a direct consequence of reading Arts' survey.

3. **Medicine — keep the heaviest, most service-dense block on the most uniform ground.** Medicine's point is that the cafeteria is the heaviest and most service-dense block and should sit on the most uniform part of the plot. **Change:** the cafeteria block is now required to be founded on a *verified* uniform stratum, or on a raft/stiffened slab where the borehole grid shows a stratum transition under it — rather than being "conventionally framed" as my survey assumed. My survey already held a raft in reserve for large-span areas; this extends that discipline explicitly to the cafeteria block.

4. **Law — ventilated metal roof over the kitchen zone.** Law notes that a ventilated metal roof over the kitchen sheds grease-laden condensate more predictably than a flat membrane roof. **Change:** the roof over the kitchen is now specified as ventilated metal, overriding my survey's general "concrete or metal standing-seam" roof line for that zone.

5. **Business — acoustic treatment in the dining hall.** Business flags that a busy dining hall of hard surfaces alone is unusable for conversation. **Change:** the dining hall now carries an explicit acoustic-treatment requirement (acoustic soffit/ceiling or equivalent), which my survey's dining palette did not include.

6. **Law — fire dampers at the compartment line, and phasing.** Law adds fire dampers where ducts cross the kitchen separation, and treats the cafeteria pavilion as a structurally and services-independent second phase. **Change:** fire dampers are now stated explicitly in §4, and the plan adopts phasing — the teaching/office terraces can be built first, with the cafeteria block following as a services-independent phase without disrupting teaching.

### 5b. Peer items that CONFIRMED decisions already made (no change)

- **Computing — grease duct, fire-rated enclosure, suppression interlock, grease interceptor, kitchen fire separation.** Already adopted in my survey; unchanged. Computing's additional open items (confirm kitchen fuel type gas vs electric; confirm planning/height limits) are added to my open-items list below, but they do not change a decision.
- **Business — no gypsum/timber in wet zones; kitchen as a high fire-hazard compartment; coved, tanked, slip-resistant floors.** Already adopted; unchanged.
- **Arts — stratified soil: bearing capacity ≠ uniformity; footprint-wide borehole grid; keep column bases on consistent strata; stiffened strip or raft across a transition; movement-tolerant slabs and drainage.** Already adopted; unchanged, and it matters more on my sloping site than on a flat one.
- **Law — separate pedestrian and service routes; cafeteria as a distinct volume with its own extract route; steel needs intumescent/board protection.** Consistent with my scheme; no change.
- **Medicine — no basement; RC frame with masonry infill; separate cafeteria wing.** Consistent with my scheme; no change. (My site is a hillside, so a cut-in semi-basement would be technically possible, but Medicine's perched-water caution and the added waterproofing/settlement risk keep me with the terraced, at-grade scheme.)
- **Computing/Business/Law/Medicine/Arts — flat-site plinth and positive-drainage strategies.** These are flat-site responses; my site is sloped, so the equivalent is the cut-off drain and terrace drainage already in §2. No change.

**Net effect:** six peer items changed or added to specific decisions (perched water; aggressive ground chemistry/made ground; cafeteria on verified uniform ground; ventilated metal roof over the kitchen; dining-hall acoustics; fire dampers and phasing). The rest confirmed decisions already made. No peer finding was rejected as inapplicable, and nothing in the peer surveys overturned the fundamental concept — the terraced stepped spine, the RC-frame-plus-selective-steel structure, and the fire-compartmented, non-combustible cafeteria all stand.

## 6. Open items for the next stage

1. Footprint-wide borehole/test-pit grid (including terrace edges, retaining lines and the cafeteria block) to establish stratum continuity, depth and thickness, groundwater and **perched** water, and **aggressive ground chemistry / made ground**.
2. Slope-stability analysis for the existing, post-construction and temporary excavation conditions.
3. Retaining-wall design for at-rest pressures with perched-water drainage behind the wall.
4. Confirm the drainage outfall capacity and invert for the terrace system.
5. Confirm kitchen fuel type (gas vs electric) — affects services, ventilation and suppression design.
6. Confirm planning/height limits, which cap the stepped-terrace massing.
7. Fire strategy sign-off for the kitchen compartmentation, grease-duct protection and fire dampers.
