# Faculty of Business — Building Plan

**Status:** Final building plan (post-survey coordination)
**Site:** Flat, level plot, approx. 60 m × 60 m (~3,600 m²)
**Soil:** Stratified (layered) profile, generally suitable for standard shallow
spread-footing foundations.
**Brief:** Lecture halls/classrooms for a mid-sized faculty, staff offices, and a
cafeteria/canteen serving students and staff.

This plan finalises the footprint/zoning and the material choices for the
Faculty of Business building. It is based on my own survey (SURVEY_BUSINESS.md)
and on a full read of the five peer surveys (Engineering, Computing, Law,
Medicine, Arts). Section 7 states explicitly what a peer's survey changed in my
plan and what it did not.

---

## 1. Basis and Method

My own survey established the site as benign: flat, level ground with stratified
soil suited to shallow spread footings, subject to a confirming geotechnical
investigation. The peer surveys fall into two groups:

- **Four flat-site faculties** (Computing, Law, Medicine, Arts) surveyed plots
  materially identical to mine — flat, level, ~60 m × 60 m, stratified soil.
  Their findings are directly transferable to my site.
- **One hillside faculty** (Engineering) surveyed an ~80 m × 100 m plot with an
  ~18 m fall. Its findings are driven by slope and are not transferable to a
  level plot (see §7.4).

I read all six surveys before finalising. Where a peer's finding was relevant to
my building, I adopted it and say so below; where it was not, I say that too.

## 2. Site and Foundation Strategy (Finalised)

### 2.1 Terrain

Flat, level ground. No cut-and-fill balancing problem, no retaining structures,
no slope-stability risk. The design consequences are the same as those
identified by my own survey and by the other flat-site faculties:

- **Positive drainage is a design responsibility, not an inherited condition.**
  A level plot does not shed water on its own.
- **Finished floor level** set a clear 300–450 mm above surrounding external
  ground to give freeboard against surface water.
- **Orientation is a free choice** on a square plot; the long axis of the
  teaching block is set roughly east–west so deep-plan lecture halls face
  north/south and glazing is controlled on the east/west elevations.

### 2.2 Foundations (finalised)

**Primary strategy: reinforced-concrete pad (spread) footings on a graded,
compacted formation, tied by a continuous reinforced ground-beam grid.**

The ground beams are not optional detailing — they are the mechanism that
distributes load and limits differential movement where the stratified layers
vary across the 60 m × 60 m plot. All footings are founded on the same competent
stratum where feasible, and column loads are kept reasonably uniform across the
grid so that no footing is loaded out of proportion to its neighbours.

**Designated fallback: a stiffened raft/mat foundation.** If the geotechnical
investigation reveals a soft or compressible layer within the footing influence
zone that cannot be economically deepened past, the pre-agreed fallback is a
raft rather than a change of foundation type under part of the building. A raft
spreads load, tolerates the modest differential settlement that layered soils
produce, and doubles as a moisture barrier under the ground floor. Abrupt
changes of foundation type under one building are to be avoided.

**Geotechnical investigation to be commissioned before design freeze** —
boreholes/trial pits to below the anticipated influence zone, SPT/CPT, layer
logging, groundwater monitoring, and bearing-capacity derivation. The
"generally suitable" description is a starting assumption, not a substitute for
site-specific data.

### 2.3 Peer inputs adopted into the foundation and ground strategy

Three findings from peer surveys were **not** in my own survey and have been
adopted into this plan:

1. **Soil and groundwater chemical testing (from Computing).** Computing's
   survey specifies chemical testing (sulphate/chloride, pH) on soil and
   groundwater to select the correct cement type and concrete cover for
   durability. My own survey did not include this. It is directly relevant to my
   building — same stratified soil, same shallow footings, same buried concrete
   — so I have added it to my geotechnical brief. **This changed my plan.**
2. **Construction-stage dewatering and seasonal scheduling (from Medicine).**
   Medicine's survey notes that with a shallow or perched water table,
   dewatering during footing excavation may be needed, and that foundation works
   should be scheduled for the drier season where possible. My own survey
   treated the water table only as a design input, not as a construction-stage
   constraint. I have adopted this as a construction-planning requirement.
   **This changed my plan.**
3. **Raft as a moisture barrier (from Law).** Law's survey makes the point that
   a raft foundation also acts as a moisture barrier under the ground floor.
   This is a genuine additional benefit that strengthens the case for the raft
   fallback already described in §2.2, and it is now recorded as one of the
   reasons for that fallback. **This refined a decision I had already made.**

## 3. Footprint and Zoning (Finalised)

### 3.1 Massing

A **compact, 2–3 storey block** within the plot. A 60 m × 60 m plot with a
mid-sized faculty brief does not support a sprawling low-rise scheme: a compact
block preserves external space for circulation, drainage, service access and
amenity, and keeps the cafeteria at ground level with a short, legible route
from the main entrance. Indicative footprint ~1,400–1,800 m² (roughly 40–50% of
the plot).

### 3.2 Zoning

```
        NORTH (quiet / academic)
  +-------------------------------------------+
  |  Lecture halls / classrooms (block)       |
  |  - large-span teaching spaces             |
  |  - stacked over 2-3 storeys               |
  +---------------------+---------------------+
  |  Staff offices      |  Vertical core:     |
  |  (cellular, quieter)|  stairs, lifts,     |
  |                     |  WCs, services      |
  +---------------------+---------------------+
  |  Cafeteria / canteen + servery + kitchen  |
  |  (ground floor, direct external access)   |
  +-------------------------------------------+
        SOUTH (public / social, entrance side)
```

**Zoning principles (finalised):**

- **Cafeteria at ground level**, on the public/entrance side, with direct
  external access and its own service/kitchen entrance. Deliveries, waste and
  grease/odour management are kept away from teaching spaces, and hygiene and
  fire separation are simplified.
- **Lecture halls/classrooms** grouped together for acoustic and scheduling
  efficiency; large-span spaces placed where structure is most efficient.
- **Staff offices** in a quieter zone, cellular or small-group, away from the
  cafeteria's noise and odours.
- **Vertical core** (stairs, lift, WCs, risers) centralised to serve all zones
  and to act as a fire-separation and evacuation spine, with two means of
  escape.
- **Circulation** kept simple and legible, with a clear main entrance and a
  separate service route.

### 3.3 Structural grid

A regular **7.5 m × 7.5 m** column grid, uniform across the footprint. Grid
regularity is the single biggest lever on construction cost and speed, and it
keeps the foundation layout uniform — which matters on stratified ground.

## 4. Primary Construction Materials — Building Generally (Finalised)

| Element | Final choice | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Foundations | RC pad footings + continuous ground beams (raft fallback) | Suits shallow spread-footing soil; ground beams control differential settlement across stratified layers. |
| Structural frame | Reinforced-concrete frame (columns, beams, slabs) | Durable, inherently fire-resistant, good acoustic mass for teaching spaces, economical, locally conventional. |
| Floors | Reinforced-concrete slabs (suspended) | Robust, acoustic mass, fire resistance, service integration. |
| External walls | Insulated masonry (block/brick) or insulated concrete/rendered systems | Durable, low maintenance, good thermal and acoustic performance. |
| Roof | RC slab roof, or a steel/timber-trussed roof over large-span halls | Concrete gives durability and fire performance; lightweight roof suits long-span teaching halls. |
| Windows/glazing | Thermally broken aluminium or timber-aluminium frames, double glazing, solar control on exposed elevations | Daylight, thermal comfort, low maintenance. |
| Internal partitions | Blockwork / drywall systems | Flexibility for office and classroom layouts. |

**Why a concrete frame as the default:** it is fire-resistant without added
protection, has the acoustic mass that teaching spaces need, tolerates the
moderate spans involved, and is straightforward to build on shallow footings.
Steel remains a viable alternative for the large-span teaching halls, where
speed or span dominates, but concrete is the baseline for the building as a
whole.

## 5. Cafeteria / Canteen — Finalised Material Choices

The cafeteria is the most demanding space in the brief: it combines large open
spans, fire separation, hygiene and moisture resistance, and long-term
maintenance in one room. The finalised specification:

### 5.1 Structure and span

- **Reinforced-concrete frame** on the same 7.5 m grid as the rest of the
  building, with long-span beams or a waffle/ribbed slab to give a column-free
  dining area. Concrete's mass also damps footfall and trolley vibration.
- **Structural steel with a composite (steel deck + concrete) floor** is the
  accepted alternative where the longest clear spans are wanted; if used, the
  steel is fire-protected with intumescent coating or fire-rated board.
- **Kitchen:** reinforced slab on grade with a durable, seamless floor finish
  and adequate load capacity for equipment.

### 5.2 Fire

- **60–120 minute fire-rated compartment separation** between the kitchen and
  the dining/academic zones, with fire-rated doors and dampers in ductwork.
  Final rating to be confirmed against the applicable building code and the
  building's overall fire strategy.
- **Concrete and masonry** are used for the compartment boundaries and
  structure because they achieve the rating inherently and robustly, rather
  than depending on a lining that can be penetrated or degraded.
- **Kitchen extract ductwork** fire-rated and grease-tight, with access for
  cleaning.
- **Means of escape** from the dining area direct and generous, given high
  occupancy.

### 5.3 Hygiene and moisture

- **Floors:** seamless, impervious, slip-resistant finish — epoxy or resin
  screed, or ceramic/quarry tiling — with **coved skirting** at the floor/wall
  junction so there is no dirt trap. Falls to trapped drainage.
- **Walls:** glazed ceramic tiling or hygienic wall cladding/panels to splash
  height, and full height in wet/kitchen zones, on a moisture-resistant
  substrate. No porous finishes.
- **Ceilings:** moisture-resistant, cleanable, with access panels for services.
- **Services:** stainless-steel worktops and equipment; sealed, washable
  junctions; mechanical extract with make-up air to control humidity, grease
  and odours.
- **Waterproofing:** wet areas and the kitchen floor tanked/waterproofed, with
  falls to trapped drainage.

### 5.4 Maintenance

- Durable, cleanable, low-maintenance surfaces (tiling, resin floors, stainless
  steel, sealed concrete) to reduce lifecycle cost in a heavy-use, wet
  environment.
- Accessible service zones and ducts so kitchen extract, drainage and plumbing
  can be maintained without disrupting teaching spaces.
- Cafeteria plant and services zoned and separated from the academic areas.

### 5.5 Cafeteria summary

| Element | Final choice |
|---|---|
| Dining-hall structure | RC frame with long-span beams / ribbed slab (protected steel + composite as the long-span alternative) |
| Kitchen structure | RC frame + reinforced slab on grade |
| Fire | 60–120 min rated compartment separation; concrete/masonry boundaries; rated grease-tight extract |
| Floors | Seamless epoxy/resin or tiled, coved, falls to trapped drainage |
| Walls | Glazed ceramic tile / hygienic cladding (full height in kitchen) |
| Ceilings | Moisture-resistant, cleanable, access panels |
| Kitchen fit-out | Stainless steel |
| Moisture | Tanked wet areas, mechanical extract, sealed junctions |

## 6. Site-Level Services and Drainage (Finalised)

- **Positive drainage design:** minimum 1:60 fall on external paving away from
  the building, perimeter land drains, and a designed attenuation/soakaway
  strategy sized to local rainfall intensity. On flat ground a blocked gully
  becomes a flood.
- **Damp-proof membrane** under the slab and **damp-proof course** in walls;
  tanked or drained protection where the water table is high.
- **Single controlled vehicle entry** with a lay-by for deliveries and refuse,
  kept clear of the pedestrian route to the cafeteria. A perimeter service loop
  is not achievable on a 60 m square with a large footprint.
- **Construction-stage dewatering** and drier-season scheduling of foundation
  excavation (adopted from Medicine — see §7.2).

## 7. Cross-Faculty Review — What Changed and What Did Not

This section is the explicit record required by the brief. I read all six
surveys. Below, each peer finding that could plausibly bear on my building is
assessed, and I state plainly whether it changed a decision.

### 7.1 Computing — chemical testing of soil and groundwater: **CHANGED MY PLAN**

Computing's survey specifies chemical testing (sulphate/chloride, pH) on soil
and groundwater to select the correct cement type and concrete cover for
durability. My own survey did not include this. Because my building sits on the
same stratified soil with the same buried concrete, this is directly relevant,
and I have added it to my geotechnical brief (§2.3). This is a genuine change:
without reading Computing's survey I would have specified a default cement and
cover without a durability basis.

### 7.2 Medicine — construction-stage dewatering and seasonal scheduling: **CHANGED MY PLAN**

Medicine's survey notes that with a shallow or perched water table, dewatering
during footing excavation may be needed, and that foundation works should be
scheduled for the drier season where possible. My own survey treated the water
table purely as a design input. I have adopted Medicine's construction-stage
constraint into my plan (§2.3, §6). This is a genuine change to how the
foundation works are planned, not just to how they are designed.

### 7.3 Law — raft foundation as a moisture barrier: **REFINED A DECISION**

Law's survey makes the point that a raft/mat foundation also acts as a moisture
barrier under the ground floor. I had already identified a raft as a possible
fallback; Law's observation gives that fallback an additional, independent
benefit and is now recorded as one of the reasons for it (§2.2). This is a
refinement of a decision I had already made, not a reversal.

### 7.4 Engineering — hillside terrace strategy: **NO CHANGE**

Engineering's survey is the only one on a different site type: an ~80 m × 100 m
hillside plot with an ~18 m fall. Its central moves — stepping the building into
terraces, retaining walls, cut-and-fill balancing per terrace, cut-off drains
above the works, slope-stability assessment, and piled/deep foundations on the
fill side — are all consequences of that fall. My plot is flat and level, so
none of these apply, and none of them changed my plan. I checked specifically
for anything transferable and found nothing that is not already covered by my
own survey: Engineering's cafeteria specification (hygienic, tanked, rated,
separately-compartmented kitchen) is consistent with mine but adds nothing my
own survey did not already contain, and its slope-specific drainage and
foundation requirements have no counterpart on a level plot. I am recording this
as a genuine "no change" rather than manufacturing a connection.

### 7.5 Computing — rejection of the lightweight drywall/vinyl cafeteria package: **NO CHANGE (corroboration only)**

Computing's survey works through, and rejects, a lightweight steel-frame /
drywall / suspended-grid-ceiling / vinyl-or-carpet cafeteria fit-out on
functional grounds: paper-faced board is not washable and supports mould, a fire
rating contingent on an intact lining is the wrong kind of rating in a cooking
environment, and drywall in a wet, trolley-bumped space is a recurring
maintenance cost. My own survey reached the same destination — concrete and
masonry with impervious, washable, coved finishes — but by a different route: I
went straight to the concrete/masonry specification without first testing the
lightweight package against food-service constraints. Computing's analysis
therefore **corroborates** my choice and strengthens the reasoning behind it,
but it did not change a decision, because I had already made the same one. I am
stating this honestly rather than claiming a change that did not occur.

### 7.6 Arts — flat-site survey: **NO CHANGE**

Arts' survey covers a plot materially identical to mine and reaches the same
conclusions (shallow footings on ground beams, compact low-rise block, cafeteria
at ground level on the public side, concrete/masonry with hygienic finishes). It
contains nothing that was not already in my own survey, and it changed no
decision.

## 8. Risks and Open Items

1. **Stratified soil** — differential settlement is the principal geotechnical
   risk. Mitigated by founding on a consistent stratum, a tied ground-beam grid,
   uniform column loads, and a raft fallback. To be confirmed by investigation.
2. **Water table** — depth and seasonal fluctuation to be established; drives
   both design (tanking, DPM) and construction planning (dewatering, seasonal
   scheduling).
3. **Concrete durability** — cement type and cover to be fixed by soil and
   groundwater chemical testing (adopted from Computing).
4. **Drainage outfall** — attenuation/soakaway capacity and the discharge point
   to be confirmed; a level plot has little natural infiltration.
5. **Fire strategy** — final fire-resistance ratings, compartmentation and
   means of escape to be confirmed against the applicable code.
6. **Cafeteria services** — grease extract, drainage falls and hygiene detailing
   to be coordinated early; they drive the kitchen layout and floor build-up.
7. **Cost** — the concrete-frame option to be cost-planned against a protected
   steel alternative for the long-span areas.

## 9. Conclusion

The Faculty of Business site is favourable: level ground and stratified soil
generally suited to shallow spread footings make a conventional
reinforced-concrete building the sensible baseline, subject to a confirming
geotechnical investigation. The building is a compact 2–3 storey block on a
regular 7.5 m grid, with the cafeteria at ground level on the public/entrance
side, teaching spaces grouped for acoustic efficiency, and offices in a quieter
zone. The primary structure is a reinforced-concrete frame with masonry infill
on tied pad-and-ground-beam foundations, with a raft as the designated fallback.

The cafeteria is built in reinforced concrete and masonry with impervious,
washable, coved finishes, 60–120 minute fire compartmentation, a separately
compartmented kitchen with rated grease-tight extract, and tanked wet areas.

Reading the five peer surveys changed two decisions and refined a third: I
adopted Computing's soil/groundwater chemical testing for cement type and
concrete cover, adopted Medicine's construction-stage dewatering and
drier-season scheduling of foundation works, and strengthened my raft fallback
on the basis of Law's observation that a raft also acts as a moisture barrier.
Engineering's hillside strategy did not apply to my level plot, and Computing's
rejection of a lightweight drywall cafeteria package corroborated a choice I had
already made rather than changing it. Arts' survey contained nothing new. Those
are the honest results of the cross-faculty review.

**Status: complete.**
