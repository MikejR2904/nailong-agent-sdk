# Faculty of Law — Initial Site & Feasibility Survey

**Status:** Independent early feasibility survey (planning phase)
**Site:** Flat, level plot, approx. 60 m × 60 m (~3,600 m²)
**Soil:** Stratified (layered) soil, generally suitable for standard shallow
spread-footing foundations.

---

## 1. Approach to Terrain and Site Conditions

### 1.1 Site character
The plot is flat and level, which is favourable: no significant cut-and-fill,
no retaining structures, and straightforward drainage by gravity. The main
terrain risks are therefore not topographic but **subsurface and hydrological**,
driven by the stratified soil profile.

### 1.2 Geotechnical approach
- **Confirm stratification before design.** "Generally suitable for shallow
  spread footings" is a preliminary statement. I would commission a
  geotechnical investigation (boreholes to ~10–15 m, SPT/CPT, groundwater
  monitoring) to establish:
  - the depth and thickness of each stratum and its bearing capacity;
  - whether any soft/compressible or expansive layer underlies the founding
    level (differential settlement risk across a large footprint);
  - the water table depth and its seasonal fluctuation.
- **Founding strategy.** Assume shallow reinforced-concrete pad and strip
  footings bearing on a competent stratum. Where a weak layer sits close to
  the surface, either deepen footings to the competent layer, or use a
  **raft/mat foundation** — attractive here because a raft spreads load and
  tolerates the modest differential settlement that layered soils can produce,
  and it also acts as a moisture barrier under the ground floor.
- **Differential settlement control.** Because the soil is layered, I would
  design for differential rather than total settlement: stiffen the structure
  with a continuous ground beam/raft grid and avoid abrupt changes in
  foundation type under one building.

### 1.3 Water, drainage and moisture
- **Surface water:** shape the external levels to a gentle fall away from the
  building; perimeter French drains / slot drains; permeable paving where
  practical.
- **Ground moisture:** a continuous **damp-proof membrane (DPM)** under the
  slab and **damp-proof course (DPC)** in walls; tanked or drained protection
  where the water table is high.
- **Stormwater:** on-site attenuation (soakaway or attenuation tank) sized to
  the local rainfall intensity, since a fully paved 60 × 60 plot has little
  natural infiltration.

### 1.4 Climate and orientation (generic temperate assumption)
- Orient long facades to manage solar gain; shade west/south glazing.
- Locate the cafeteria and social spaces to catch daylight without excessive
  heat gain; buffer lecture halls from the noisiest site edge.

## 2. Rough Zoning and Footprint Concept

### 2.1 Massing
A single compact **2–3 storey block** is preferred over sprawling low-rise:
it minimises the footprint (leaving room for circulation, landscaping and
future expansion), shortens service runs, and reduces roof area and heat loss.
Indicative footprint: **~1,400–1,800 m²** (roughly 40–50% of the plot),
leaving the remainder for entrances, parking/drop-off, planting and
stormwater attenuation.

### 2.2 Zoning concept (indicative)
| Zone | Function | Notes |
|------|----------|-------|
| **A — Public/entry** | Entrance foyer, reception, admin, dean's office | Street-facing, clear wayfinding |
| **B — Teaching** | Lecture halls, seminar rooms, classrooms | Quiet side; stacked for acoustic separation |
| **C — Staff** | Offices, meeting rooms, staff room | Adjacent to teaching but acoustically buffered |
| **D — Social/cafeteria** | Canteen, servery, kitchen, seating | Ground floor, own entrance, near core |
| **E — Core/service** | Stairs, lifts, WCs, plant, storage | Central spine, repeated per floor |

### 2.3 Circulation and access
- Central circulation spine with a vertical core (stairs + lift) serving all
  floors; two means of escape for fire compliance.
- Separate **servicing access** for the kitchen (deliveries, waste) kept away
  from the main student entrance.
- Accessible (step-free) entrance and WCs throughout.

### 2.4 Flexibility
- Regular structural grid and demountable partitions so classrooms can be
  reconfigured; generous floor-to-floor height for services and future
  adaptation.

## 3. Primary Construction Materials — General Building

**Primary structural system: reinforced concrete frame with masonry infill.**
Rationale: the layered soil and the desire for a stiff, settlement-tolerant
structure favour a rigid concrete frame on a raft/pad foundation; concrete
also gives good fire resistance, acoustic mass (important for lecture halls),
and long service life with low maintenance.

- **Frame:** in-situ reinforced concrete columns and beams (or flat slabs for
  flexibility and lower floor-to-floor height).
- **Floors:** reinforced concrete slabs — good acoustic separation between
  stacked teaching floors and inherent fire resistance.
- **External walls:** clay/concrete block masonry with a ventilated cavity,
  insulation and a rainscreen or render finish; good thermal mass and
  durability.
- **Roof:** insulated flat roof (warm-deck) with a durable membrane
  (single-ply or built-up), designed for maintenance access and future PV.
- **Windows/glazing:** thermally broken aluminium or timber-aluminium frames,
  double/triple glazed, with solar control on exposed elevations.
- **Internal partitions:** lightweight drywall/blockwork, demountable where
  flexibility is needed.
- **Finishes:** durable, low-maintenance, hard-wearing (relevant to a busy
  faculty).

**Alternative considered:** a steel frame with composite deck floors would
speed erection and give long spans, but offers less inherent fire and acoustic
mass and needs additional fire protection — so concrete is preferred as the
primary system, with steel used selectively where long spans are needed.

## 4. Materials Specifically for the Cafeteria / Canteen

The cafeteria is the most demanding zone: it needs **long clear spans** (open
seating), **fire separation** from the kitchen and the rest of the building,
**hygiene and moisture resistance** (steam, grease, washing-down), and **low
maintenance** in a high-traffic, wet environment.

### 4.1 Structural span
- Provide a **long-span floor/roof over the seating area** to avoid columns in
  the dining space. Options: a reinforced-concrete flat slab with a wider grid,
  or a **steel/composite beam** system where a longer, lighter span is
  advantageous. A regular grid of ~8–9 m is a reasonable target.
- Kitchen and servery can use the standard concrete frame grid.

### 4.2 Fire rating
- The cafeteria/kitchen is a higher fire-risk area (cooking equipment, grease).
  Provide **compartmentation**: fire-rated walls and floors (typically 60–120
  min) separating the kitchen from the dining area and the rest of the
  building, with fire-rated doors and dampers in ductwork.
- Concrete and masonry give inherent fire resistance; where steel is used,
  apply intumescent protection or board cladding to achieve the required
  rating.

### 4.3 Hygiene and moisture resistance
- **Floors:** slip-resistant, seamless, coved **hygienic resin or tiled**
  finish with falls to drainage; impervious and easy to clean.
- **Walls (kitchen/servery):** smooth, washable, impervious surfaces — glazed
  ceramic tiles or hygienic wall cladding to splashback height, sealed joints.
- **Ceilings:** moisture-resistant, wipe-clean panels; avoid porous finishes.
- **Services:** stainless-steel kitchen equipment, grease traps, dedicated
  extract with filtration, and easy-clean ductwork.
- **Vapour/moisture control:** robust ventilation and extraction to manage
  steam and condensation; sealed, moisture-resistant construction.

### 4.4 Maintenance
- Choose durable, replaceable, easy-clean materials: stainless steel, glazed
  tile, resin flooring, sealed concrete. Design for access to services
  (ceiling voids, service risers) so maintenance does not disrupt teaching.
- External: hard-wearing, easily cleaned finishes at ground level.

### 4.5 Summary of cafeteria material choices
| Element | Choice | Why |
|---------|--------|-----|
| Structure | RC frame; steel/composite for long spans | Clear spans, fire mass |
| Fire separation | 60–120 min rated walls/floors, rated doors | Kitchen fire risk |
| Floor | Slip-resistant seamless resin / tiled, coved | Hygiene, moisture, safety |
| Walls | Glazed tile / hygienic cladding | Washable, impervious |
| Ceiling | Moisture-resistant wipe-clean panels | Hygiene, condensation |
| Services | Stainless steel, dedicated extract + grease trap | Hygiene, fire, maintenance |

## 5. Feasibility Assessment and Next Steps

**Feasibility: positive.** The flat site and generally suitable stratified soil
support a conventional shallow-foundation, concrete-frame building. The main
technical attention points are (a) confirming the stratification and water
table by geotechnical investigation, (b) designing for differential settlement
(raft/stiffened foundation), and (c) robust moisture, drainage and
compartmentation detailing — especially for the cafeteria.

**Recommended next steps**
1. Commission geotechnical and topographical surveys.
2. Confirm local planning, fire and accessibility requirements.
3. Develop the zoning concept into a schematic layout with a structural grid.
4. Size stormwater attenuation and confirm service connections.
5. Cost-plan the concrete-frame option against a steel alternative.

*This is an independent early feasibility survey; it does not reflect the
other faculties' surveys and is subject to confirmation by detailed
investigation and design.*
