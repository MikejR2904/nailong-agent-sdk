# Faculty of Medicine — Final Building Plan

**Status:** Final plan (post-coordination). Supersedes the initial feasibility
position in `SURVEY_MEDICINE.md`.
**Site:** Flat, level plot, approx. 60 m × 60 m (~3,600 m²).
**Soil:** Stratified (layered) profile, generally suitable for standard shallow
spread-footing foundations.
**Brief:** Lecture halls/classrooms for a mid-sized faculty, staff offices, and
a cafeteria/canteen serving students and staff.

**Inputs read:** `SURVEY_ENGINEERING.md`, `SURVEY_COMPUTING.md`,
`SURVEY_BUSINESS.md`, `SURVEY_LAW.md`, `SURVEY_MEDICINE.md`, `SURVEY_ARTS.md`.

---

## 1. Site Approach (finalized)

The site is flat and level with a stratified soil profile. Nothing in any peer
survey changes this reading, and no peer survey reports a site condition that
applies to my plot — four of the five peers (Computing, Business, Law, Arts)
are on materially identical flat 60 m × 60 m plots, and the fifth (Engineering)
is on a hillside, which is a different site type entirely.

**Finalized approach:**

1. **Confirm the stratification before design freeze.** Commission a
   geotechnical investigation — boreholes/trial pits to below the footing
   influence zone, SPT/CPT, groundwater monitoring over a wet season where
   possible, and sulphate/chloride/pH chemical testing to fix cement type and
   concrete cover. "Generally suitable" is a starting assumption, not data.
2. **Design for differential settlement, not just bearing capacity.** Found all
   footings on the same competent stratum; use a stiff, tied foundation system
   (interconnected pad footings with tie beams, or a raft where a weak layer
   sits close to formation) and keep column loads uniform across the grid.
3. **Drainage and moisture.** Set finished floor levels 300–450 mm above
   surrounding external ground; positive falls away from the building;
   perimeter land drains; DPM under the slab and DPC in walls; tanking to any
   below-grade or moisture-sensitive area.
4. **Stormwater attenuation** sized to local rainfall intensity, since a fully
   paved 60 m × 60 m plot has little natural infiltration.
5. **Excavation and groundwater.** Keep excavations shallow; dewater if a
   shallow water table is confirmed; schedule foundation works for the drier
   season where possible.
6. **Lateral robustness.** Continuous tied foundation and a regular structural
   layout to avoid torsional irregularity.

**Conclusion:** feasible for a conventional shallow-foundation building. The
principal geotechnical risks remain differential settlement across layers and
moisture/drainage on flat ground.

## 2. Footprint and Zoning (finalized)

**Massing:** a compact, low-rise (2–3 storey) block, roughly rectangular,
occupying the central portion of the plot and leaving a perimeter band for
circulation, planting, and services.

**Footprint concept:** an **L-shaped or two-bar (linear) plan** rather than a
deep single block — it keeps spans and structural depth economical, gives
daylight and cross-ventilation to both wings, and lets the cafeteria sit at the
"public" corner near the main entrance and service yard, separated from quiet
teaching zones by the circulation core.

**Zoning shares (indicative):**

| Zone | Approx. share of plot | Contents |
|------|----------------------|----------|
| Building footprint | ~40–45% | Academic block + cafeteria wing |
| Circulation / entrances / plazas | ~15% | Main entry, drop-off, accessible routes |
| Service / deliveries / plant | ~10% | Kitchen service yard, waste, mechanical |
| Landscape / open space / future expansion | ~30–35% | Green space, seating, expansion reserve |

**Indicative arrangement:**

- **Ground floor:** main entrance/lobby, cafeteria + kitchen at the public
  corner with its own service access, a large lecture hall, admin/reception.
- **Upper floors:** classrooms, seminar rooms, staff offices, faculty support
  spaces.
- **Vertical core:** stairs + lift + services, positioned to serve both wings
  and act as a fire-separated escape route.

**Structural grid:** a regular, repetitive column grid of **~7.5–9 m bays** to
suit both teaching spaces and office modules and to keep the foundation layout
uniform.

**Orientation:** the plot is effectively square, so orientation is a free
choice. Set the long axis of the teaching block roughly east–west so deep-plan
lecture halls face north/south and glazing is controlled on the east/west
elevations (low sun = glare and heat gain in a lecture theatre).

**Servicing:** a perimeter service loop is not achievable on a 60 m square with
a large footprint. Plan a single controlled vehicle entry with a lay-by for
deliveries and refuse, kept clear of the pedestrian route to the cafeteria, and
route the kitchen extract to the service yard (see §4).

## 3. Primary Construction Materials — Building Generally (finalized)

**Structure:** **reinforced concrete (RC) frame** — cast-in-situ columns,
beams, and slabs. Rationale: well matched to a tied shallow-foundation system
on layered soil; inherent fire resistance and acoustic mass for teaching
spaces; locally conventional, robust, low-maintenance; flexible for the regular
grid and future fit-out.

- **Slabs:** RC two-way or one-way slabs for typical floors; a **waffle or
  ribbed slab** where longer spans or reduced self-weight are beneficial.
- **Long-span areas (large lecture halls):** deeper RC beams, or a structural
  steel roof/frame where clear spans are needed — with explicit fire protection
  (see §4.1).

**Foundations:** reinforced-concrete pad/strip footings on ground beams, or a
raft where the investigation indicates a weak layer near formation.

**Envelope:**

- **External walls:** insulated masonry (block/brick) or RC with infill
  masonry — durable, good thermal and acoustic mass.
- **Cladding/facade:** rainscreen or rendered/insulated finish with shading
  devices appropriate to orientation.
- **Roof:** insulated, well-drained warm flat roof (RC, or lightweight metal
  deck over steel where spans are long) with a durable waterproof membrane,
  designed for maintenance access and future PV.
- **Glazing:** thermally broken, double-glazed units with solar control.

**Interiors and services:** durable, low-maintenance finishes in high-traffic
areas; acoustic treatment in lecture halls and classrooms; fire-rated
compartmentation and protected escape routes throughout.

## 4. Cafeteria / Canteen — Finalized Material Choices

The cafeteria has distinct demands: **structural span** (large open dining
area), **fire rating**, **hygiene**, **moisture resistance**, and
**maintenance**. These drive the choices below.

### 4.1 Structural span — finalized

- **Dining area:** **RC waffle/ribbed slab or post-tensioned RC** is the
  **default** for the dining hall, giving a column-light space on the same
  regular grid as the rest of the building, with concrete's mass damping
  footfall and trolley vibration.
- **Structural steel beams/trusses with a composite or metal-deck roof** are
  retained **only where the required clear span genuinely exceeds what RC can
  carry economically** — and where used, the steel **must** be fire-protected
  (intumescent coating or fire-rated board) to achieve the required rating.
  Steel is no longer the default for the dining hall (see §5, change 2).
- **Kitchen:** a **reinforced slab on grade** with a durable, seamless floor
  finish and adequate load capacity for equipment, on the standard RC frame
  with a tighter grid.

### 4.2 Fire rating — finalized

- The cafeteria/kitchen is a **higher fire-risk area** (cooking equipment,
  grease, gas). The **kitchen is a separate fire compartment** with fire-rated
  walls and doors and a rated separation from the dining and academic zones.
- **Working rating: 60–90 minutes** for structure and compartmentation,
  confirmed against the applicable code.
- **Dedicated, rated kitchen exhaust/duct system** with grease filters and
  appropriate fire dampers.
- **Suppression: sprinkler coverage to the dining hall plus dedicated kitchen
  hood suppression** — now specified explicitly rather than "where required"
  (see §5, change 1).
- **Concrete and masonry** are preferred precisely because they are inherently
  non-combustible and achieve fire ratings with minimal added treatment.

### 4.3 Hygiene — finalized

- **Walls:** smooth, washable, non-porous finishes — ceramic tiling or hygienic
  wall panels in the kitchen and food-prep areas; easy-clean painted/rendered
  surfaces in the dining area.
- **Floors:** **seamless, slip-resistant, coved** finishes (epoxy or
  welded-sheet vinyl / quarry tile) with coved skirting to prevent dirt traps
  and allow wet cleaning.
- **Ceilings:** washable, moisture-resistant, easily cleaned panels in the
  kitchen; acoustic but cleanable surfaces in the dining area.
- Design for **easy cleaning and pest control**: smooth junctions, sealed
  penetrations, and a logical separation of clean/dirty flows.
- **No paper-faced plasterboard and no carpet anywhere in the food-service
  zone** (see §5, change 3).

### 4.4 Moisture resistance — finalized

- **Moisture-resistant substrates and finishes** (cement-based boards, tiling,
  epoxy/vinyl flooring, moisture-resistant ceiling panels).
- **Robust waterproofing/tanking** to floors and wet walls.
- **Mechanical extract ventilation** to control humidity and condensation.
- **Floor falls to drains** and sealed, cleanable junctions.
- The **RC structure** itself is inherently moisture-tolerant — a further
  reason to prefer concrete in this zone.

### 4.5 Maintenance — finalized

- **Durable, repairable, easily cleaned** materials: tiling, epoxy floors,
  stainless-steel kitchen fitments, and washable wall systems.
- Services (extract ducts, drainage, water) kept **accessible** for
  maintenance.
- **Non-combustible, hard-wearing** surfaces that tolerate frequent cleaning
  and heavy use with minimal lifecycle cost.

### 4.6 Cafeteria material summary (final)

| Element | Final choice | Why |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| Dining-hall structure | **RC waffle/P-T slab** (default); steel beams + composite deck only for spans RC cannot carry economically, with intumescent/board fire protection | Clear spans, non-combustible, mass-damped |
| Kitchen structure | Reinforced slab on grade + RC frame | Heavy loads, wet environment |
| Kitchen walls | Fire-rated masonry/board + ceramic tiling | Fire rating + hygiene |
| Dining walls | Washable paint/panels, tiling at splash zones | Hygiene, durability |
| Floors | Seamless epoxy / welded vinyl / quarry tile, coved | Hygiene, moisture, slip resistance |
| Ceilings | Moisture-resistant washable panels | Hygiene, humidity |
| Kitchen fit-out | Stainless steel | Hygiene, durability, maintenance |
| Fire strategy | Separate rated kitchen compartment (60–90 min), rated ducts/dampers, **sprinklers + kitchen hood suppression** | Life safety |
| Excluded | Paper-faced plasterboard, carpet, standard office grid ceiling tiles | Functional failure in food-service conditions |

## 5. What the Peer Surveys Changed — and What They Did Not

I read all five peer surveys. Four of the five (Computing, Business, Law, Arts)
are on materially identical flat 60 m × 60 m plots with the same stratified
soil, and Engineering is on a hillside. Below I state honestly which peer
findings changed a decision I would otherwise have made, and which did not.

### Change 1 — Cafeteria fire suppression made explicit (from Engineering)

My own survey left suppression as "sprinkler/suppression coverage **where
required**". Engineering's survey, working the same cafeteria fire problem,
specifies a **separate rated kitchen compartment with its own suppression
(sprinkler + kitchen hood suppression)** as a firm requirement, not a
contingency. That is a better-specified position for a space with cooking
equipment and grease, and it is transferable regardless of site. **I have
adopted it**: the kitchen is now a separate rated compartment with dedicated
hood suppression, and the dining hall carries sprinkler coverage. This is a
genuine change to my plan, prompted by a peer's survey.

### Change 2 — Steel demoted from default to exception in the dining hall (from Engineering)

My survey proposed "structural steel for long-span lecture halls **and the
cafeteria dining area**" as a co-equal option with RC. Engineering's survey
makes the point explicitly that steel requires **intumescent coating or
fire-rated board** to achieve the required rating, whereas RC elements achieve
it inherently — and that in a high-fire-load cafeteria a rating that depends on
added protection is the weaker choice. Combined with Computing's and Law's
reasoning (concrete's inherent fire resistance and acoustic mass), I have
**changed my default**: the dining hall is now **RC waffle/post-tensioned slab
by default**, with steel retained only where the span genuinely exceeds what RC
can carry economically, and then only with explicit fire protection. This is a
change of default, not a change of palette.

### Change 3 — Explicit exclusion of the lightweight drywall/vinyl package (from Computing)

Computing's survey worked through a **lightweight steel frame + plasterboard
partitions + suspended grid ceiling + vinyl/carpet floor** cafeteria package
and rejected it on functional grounds: paper-faced board wicks moisture and
supports mould, the fire rating is contingent on an intact lining, and drywall
in a wet, trolley-bumped environment is a recurring wet-trade repair cost. My
own survey never considered that package — I had gone straight to tiled/epoxy
finishes — so this did not overturn a decision I had made. But it is a
**genuinely relevant finding from a peer's survey**, and I have acted on it by
writing the exclusion into my plan explicitly (§4.3, §4.6): **no paper-faced
plasterboard, no carpet, and no standard office grid ceiling tiles in the
food-service zone.** Stating the exclusion is a change to the plan even though
the underlying material choice was already correct.

### Change 4 — Raft foundation as a moisture barrier, and sized stormwater attenuation (from Law)

Law's survey makes two points I had not stated as clearly: that a **raft/mat
foundation doubles as a moisture barrier under the ground floor** (attractive
on layered soil where a weak layer sits near formation), and that **on-site
stormwater attenuation must be sized to local rainfall intensity** because a
fully paved 60 m × 60 m plot has little natural infiltration. Both are
site-independent and apply directly to my flat plot. **I have adopted both**
(§1.3, §1.4): the raft option is now explicitly justified partly as a moisture
barrier, and stormwater attenuation is now a sized design item rather than an
assumed provision.

### Change 5 — Kitchen extract routed to the service yard (from Arts)

Arts' survey specifies that the kitchen extract, with grease filtration and
odour control, is **ducted to the service yard**, keeping catering odour and
discharge away from teaching and office windows. My survey required a rated
extract but did not fix its discharge point. **I have adopted the routing**
(§2, §4.2): the extract discharges to the service yard, away from teaching and
office elevations.

### What did NOT change

- **Engineering's site strategy does not transfer.** Its terraced, stepped
  massing, retaining walls, cut-off drains, slope-stability assessment and
  piled foundations are all responses to an 18 m fall across its plot. My plot
  is flat and level. I have **not** imported any of that; doing so would be
  fabricating a connection that is not there. Only Engineering's
  site-independent cafeteria findings (changes 1 and 2) were relevant to me.
- **The flat-site peers corroborated rather than changed my plan.** Computing,
  Business, Law and Arts independently reached the same core position I did —
  RC frame on tied shallow foundations, compact 2–3 storey block, cafeteria at
  ground level with its own service access, and a cafeteria built in
  concrete/masonry with impervious, washable, coved finishes and inherent fire
  resistance. That convergence is reassuring but it did not alter a decision;
  my survey had already reached it. I am not claiming a peer "changed" a
  decision where they merely agreed with one.
- **My zoning, massing, structural grid and general material palette are
  unchanged** by the peer surveys.

## 6. Feasibility Summary (final)

- **Site:** Feasible. Flat ground simplifies earthworks; layered soil is
  manageable with a tied shallow-foundation system founded on a consistent
  competent stratum, subject to a confirming geotechnical investigation.
- **Key risks:** differential settlement across soil layers; drainage and
  moisture on flat ground; kitchen fire and hygiene demands.
- **Mitigations:** geotechnical investigation; tied/raft foundation (raft also
  acting as a moisture barrier); positive drainage, sized stormwater
  attenuation and waterproofing; fire-rated, non-combustible, washable
  cafeteria construction with a separately compartmented kitchen.
- **Primary structure:** reinforced concrete frame on shallow tied
  foundations, with structural steel (fire-protected) only for long spans that
  RC cannot carry economically.
- **Cafeteria:** non-combustible RC structure by default, fire-rated kitchen
  compartment with dedicated rated extract discharging to the service yard and
  sprinkler + hood suppression, and seamless, moisture-resistant, hygienic
  finishes throughout — with paper-faced board, carpet and office grid ceiling
  tiles explicitly excluded.

**Overall:** The site and brief are feasible for a mid-sized Faculty of
Medicine building. No site condition identified here is a blocker. The
principal design drivers are foundation detailing on layered soil and the
fire/hygiene/moisture performance of the cafeteria. Five peer-survey findings
changed or firmed up my plan (changes 1–5 above); the remainder of the peer
material either addressed a different site type or simply agreed with the
position I had already reached.

**Status: complete.**
