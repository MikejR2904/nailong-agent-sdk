# PLAN_ENGINEERING.md

## Faculty of Engineering — Final Building Plan

**Status:** Final plan (post-survey coordination)
**Site:** Hillside plot, ~80 m across-slope × 100 m up-slope, ~18 m fall low→high edge (~18% average grade)
**Brief:** Lecture halls/classrooms for a mid-sized faculty, staff offices, cafeteria/canteen for students and staff.
**Basis:** My own survey (SURVEY_ENGINEERING.md) plus the five peer surveys (Computing, Business, Law, Medicine, Arts), all now read.

> This plan finalises the footprint/zoning concept and the material choices from my
> feasibility survey. It is still a planning-stage position to be confirmed by
> geotechnical investigation, fire engineering and cost study — but the decisions
> below are now made, not merely proposed.

---

## 1. What the Peer Surveys Changed — and What They Did Not

I read all five peer surveys. Four of the five (Computing, Business, Law, Medicine, Arts)
are on **flat, level 60 m × 60 m plots with stratified soil** — a materially different
site from my hillside. Most of their content is therefore *not* transferable to my
building, and I have not pretended otherwise. But two peer findings are genuinely
relevant to my site, and one of them **changed a decision I would otherwise have made**.

### 1.1 CHANGE — Computing's rejection of paper-faced board in the food-service zone

**What Computing found.** Computing worked through a lightweight drywall/vinyl cafeteria
package and rejected it on functional grounds. The decisive argument was not the vinyl
floor but the **plasterboard**: even *moisture-resistant* plasterboard is a paper-faced,
gypsum-cored product. In a kitchen/servery with steam, splash and frequent washing it
wicks moisture, the paper face supports mould, and joints and screw heads become
harbourage points. Computing's conclusion: "no paper-faced board" in the food-service
zone, and a fire rating that is contingent on an intact paper-faced lining is the wrong
kind of rating.

**Why it applies to me even though I am on a different site.** This is a *material
behaviour* finding, not a *site* finding. My own survey specified, for the cafeteria
kitchen, "**moisture-resistant boards** and impervious finishes" and "ceilings (kitchen):
wipeable, **moisture-resistant panels** with sealed joints". That is exactly the
paper-faced gypsum product Computing rejected. My hillside site makes the kitchen no
less wet than Computing's flat site — if anything my kitchen is more moisture-exposed,
because it sits at the low terrace (T1) where groundwater and surface run-off collect.

**What I changed.** I have **removed paper-faced gypsum board from the entire
food-service zone** (kitchen, servery, wash-up, stores). The kitchen wall and ceiling
substrate is now **cement-based board or masonry/concrete**, finished with full-height
tiling or hygienic wall cladding; the kitchen ceiling is a **moisture-resistant,
wipeable, sealed panel system on a non-paper-faced substrate**, not a plasterboard
build-up. This is a change from my survey, and it is attributable to Computing's
finding. I would not have made it from my own survey alone, because my survey treated
"moisture-resistant board" as sufficient for a wet kitchen — Computing's argument shows
it is not.

### 1.2 CHANGE — Law's raft-as-moisture-barrier, applied at my low terrace

**What Law found.** Law proposed a **raft/mat foundation** on the stratified flat site
partly because, in addition to spreading load and tolerating differential settlement, a
raft "also acts as a moisture barrier under the ground floor."

**Why it applies to me.** My cafeteria sits at the **lowest terrace (T1)**, which my own
survey identified as "the most exposed to groundwater and surface run-off", requiring
"tanking to the below-ground walls and slab". Law's point is that the foundation itself
can be made to do part of that moisture-protection job rather than treating foundation
and tanking as two separate systems.

**What I changed.** At the T1 cafeteria terrace I have **refined the foundation strategy
from "pad/raft on the cut side" to a continuous reinforced-concrete raft slab under the
cafeteria block**, detailed as a combined structural raft and moisture barrier
(integral watertight concrete / tanked raft with a continuous DPM turned up into the
walls). This is a change in emphasis and specification from my survey, attributable to
Law's observation. It does not change the foundation strategy for the upper terraces,
where the cut-side pad/raft and fill-side deep foundations remain as surveyed.

### 1.3 NOT a change — Medicine's dewatering note (adopted as a sequencing constraint)

Medicine noted that with a shallow water table, **dewatering during footing excavation
may be needed**, and that foundation works should be scheduled for the drier season.
This is relevant to my low terrace, but it is a *construction-sequencing* constraint
rather than a change to any design decision. I have adopted it as a constraint (see
§5) and record it here so it is not lost — but I am not claiming it changed a decision.

### 1.4 NOT a change — the flat-site stratified-soil cautions

Computing, Business, Law, Medicine and Arts all warn about **differential settlement
across stratified layers** on their flat plots, and recommend tied/stiffened foundations
(ground beams, raft, uniform founding stratum). My site is a hillside, not a flat
stratified plot, and my survey already handles differential movement through the terrace
strategy, retaining-wall design and a slope-stability assessment. I have **not**
transplanted their flat-site foundation reasoning onto my slope, because the governing
geotechnical problem on my site is slope stability and cut/fill, not layer variability
under a level platform. I note the convergence but it did not change my plan.

### 1.5 NOT a change — the convergent cafeteria palette (corroboration only)

All five peers independently arrived at a cafeteria of **reinforced concrete/masonry
structure with impervious, washable, coved finishes, a rated kitchen compartment and
stainless-steel fit-out** — essentially the same palette my own survey proposed. This is
**corroboration, not a change**: it raises my confidence that the concrete/masonry +
tiled/resin route is the right one for a food-service space, but it did not alter a
decision I had already made. I am stating this explicitly rather than dressing up
agreement as a discovery.

## 2. Footprint and Zoning

The site is a hillside with an **18 m fall over 100 m up-slope (~18% average grade)**.
The plan is unchanged in principle from my survey: **terrace the building into the
slope; do not flatten it.** A single level platform would demand enormous cut at the
uphill edge, enormous fill at the downhill edge, a high retaining structure and a large
unstable fill mass. I reject it.

### 2.1 Massing

- **Stepped spine running up-slope**, 3–4 terraces, each a level floor plate of roughly
  4–6 m fall, with **local cut-and-fill balanced per terrace** to minimise imported fill
  and exported spoil.
- Footprint **narrow up-slope** (shallow cut/fill band per terrace) and **long
  across-slope** (using the 80 m width), keeping retaining heights moderate and daylight
  reaching every floor plate.
- **Central circulation spine** (stairs + lifts + concourse) running across-slope,
  connecting the terraces and doubling as the accessible vertical route.
- **Courtyards / light wells** cut into the terraces between wings for daylight and air.
- **Roofs step with the terraces**, giving usable plant terraces and reducing apparent
  height against the hill.
- Order of magnitude: **8,000–12,000 m² gross floor area**, i.e. roughly **2,500–3,500 m²
  per terrace band** — comfortably within the 80 m × 100 m plot once circulation,
  courtyards and access are allowed for. To be refined against the room schedule.

### 2.2 Zoning by terrace

| Terrace | Level (relative) | Function | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 (low edge) | ±0 m | **Cafeteria/canteen**, service yard, deliveries, parking | Heaviest servicing, most public, easiest vehicle access, best daylight and views; keeps food deliveries off steep ramps. |
| T2 | ~ −4 to −5 m | **Lecture halls / large classrooms**, main student concourse, main entrance from mid-slope | High-traffic teaching near the main pedestrian arrival; large-span halls suit a terrace with a deep cut behind. |
| T3 | ~ −9 to −10 m | **Classrooms, seminar rooms, teaching labs** | Mid-slope, quieter, flexible room sizes. |
| T4 (high edge) | ~ −14 to −18 m | **Staff offices, administration, library/study** | Quietest, best views, least public traffic; offices benefit from the stable cut side. |

### 2.3 Access and circulation

- **Vehicles:** a single access road climbing the slope at a controlled gradient,
  entering at the low edge and serving each terrace. Service/delivery access to the
  cafeteria is at T1, keeping heavy vehicles off the steep upper ramps.
- **Pedestrians:** stepped terraces and stairs as the primary vertical movement, with
  **lifts connecting all levels**. The accessible route is a series of ramps at compliant
  gradients (broken into landings) or, where the fall is too great, by lift. I do **not**
  rely on a single long ramp at 18%.
- **Parking** at the low (downhill) edge where the ground is closest to level.

### 2.4 Retaining, drainage and slope stability

- **Retaining walls** at each terrace edge: reinforced-concrete cantilever or counterfort
  walls for the taller steps (up to ~5–6 m), with **soil-nailed or anchored** solutions
  where the wall must also stabilise the slope behind it. Wall type to be confirmed by
  geotechnical investigation.
- **Drainage-first design:** an interception (cut-off) drain above the uphill edge of the
  works; surface-water channels and a below-ground drainage blanket behind every
  retaining wall to relieve hydrostatic pressure; and a positive gravity outfall to the
  low edge. No water ponds against a wall or sits under a floor slab.
- **Slope stability:** the geotechnical brief must establish the existing factor of
  safety, the effect of cutting the toe and loading the terraces, and whether ground
  improvement, anchoring or piling is needed. **No construction starts before a
  slope-stability assessment is complete.**

## 3. Finalised Material Choices — Building Generally

**Primary structure: in-situ reinforced-concrete (RC) frame with a masonry/concrete
envelope; steel used selectively for long-span roofs.** (Unchanged from my survey.)

- **RC frame** integrates naturally with the retaining walls and foundations, handles the
  irregular grid the terraces impose, and gives the mass and stiffness needed where
  floors step and where walls retain soil. Good inherent fire performance and durability.
- **Foundations:** pad/raft on the cut side and piled or deep foundations on the fill
  side, confirmed by geotechnical investigation — **except at T1, where a continuous
  raft is now adopted** (§1.2).
- **Steel** for long-span lecture-hall roofs and large column-free spans, protected with
  intumescent coating or fire-rated board to the required rating.
- **External walls:** insulated cavity masonry or concrete with a rainscreen/cladding
  finish — robust, low-maintenance, tolerant of a damp hillside environment.
- **Roofs:** warm flat roofs with a durable single-ply membrane and generous drainage
  falls; stepped roofs drained to the terrace below.
- **Glazing:** thermally broken aluminium or timber-aluminium frames, double/triple
  glazed, with shading to control solar gain on the exposed slope.
- **Internal partitions:** blockwork and dry-lining systems; **no paper-faced board in
  wet or food-service areas** (§1.1).

**Why not a lightweight/timber-primary building here?** Timber remains attractive for
carbon and speed and I would use it for internal partitions, some roofs and fit-out — but
a slope site with heavy retaining and foundation demands, plus the fire and robustness
requirements of a public teaching building, make an RC primary frame the lower-risk
choice. A hybrid RC/timber scheme is worth testing at the next stage.

## 4. Finalised Material Choices — Cafeteria / Canteen

The cafeteria sits at the lowest terrace (T1) and carries the most demanding combination
of requirements: **structural span, fire rating, hygiene, moisture resistance and
maintenance**. The following is finalised.

### 4.1 Structure and span

- **Dining hall:** **steel portal/truss roof on RC columns/walls**, giving ~12–18 m clear
  spans without deep RC beams. Steel members protected by intumescent coating or
  fire-rated board.
- **Kitchen and servery:** standard RC frame on a tighter grid.
- **Floor:** reinforced-concrete slab with a fall to drains. **At T1 the slab is founded
  on a continuous RC raft detailed as a combined structural raft and moisture barrier**
  (integral watertight concrete / tanked raft, DPM turned up into the walls) — the change
  from §1.2.

### 4.2 Fire

- Working assumption **60–90 minutes** for structure and compartmentation, confirmed
  against the applicable code. RC elements achieve the rating inherently; steel roof
  members are protected.
- **Kitchen is a separate fire compartment** with rated walls/doors and its own
  suppression (assumed sprinkler + kitchen hood suppression). Rated, grease-tight kitchen
  extract ductwork with grease filters and fire dampers, ducted to the service yard.

### 4.3 Hygiene — the decisive driver

- **Floors:** seamless, non-slip, coved **epoxy or resin screed** in kitchen, servery and
  wash-up; durable, hard-wearing, cleanable finish in the dining area. Coved skirting to
  walls so there are no dirt traps.
- **Walls (kitchen/servery/wash-up):** **cement-based board or masonry/concrete
  substrate** with **full-height ceramic/porcelain tiling or hygienic wall cladding**,
  food-grade sealed joints. **No paper-faced gypsum board anywhere in the food-service
  zone** — the change from §1.1.
- **Ceilings (kitchen):** moisture-resistant, wipeable, sealed panel system on a
  **non-paper-faced substrate**; services kept out of food areas or fully enclosed.
- **Dining area:** durable, scrubbable finishes — hard-wearing cleanable flooring and
  painted or clad walls. No carpet, no paper-faced board.

### 4.4 Moisture resistance

- **Moisture-resistant substrates and impervious finishes** throughout the wet zone,
  sealed penetrations, and **mechanical extract with make-up air** to control humidity and
  grease-laden vapour.
- Floor build-ups include a **tanked/waterproofed layer** and trapped drainage.
- Being at the low terrace, the cafeteria is the most exposed to groundwater and surface
  run-off: **tanking to the below-ground walls and slab**, a perimeter drainage channel,
  and a floor level set above the external ground/outfall. The **raft-as-moisture-barrier**
  (§1.2) is the first line of this protection.

### 4.5 Maintenance

- All finishes chosen for **easy repair and cleaning**: replaceable tile/cladding panels,
  resin floors that can be re-coated, accessible service voids, robust cleanable surfaces.
- **Stainless steel** for kitchen equipment and worktops (hygienic, corrosion-resistant,
  long-lived).

### 4.6 Finalised cafeteria materials

| Element | Final choice |
|---|---|
| Dining-hall structure | Steel portal/truss roof on RC columns, 12–18 m clear span |
| Kitchen structure | RC frame, tighter grid |
| T1 foundation | Continuous RC raft, detailed as combined structural raft + moisture barrier |
| Fire | 60–90 min rated structure/compartmentation; separate rated kitchen compartment; sprinklers + hood suppression; rated grease-tight extract |
| Floors | Coved, seamless resin/epoxy (kitchen/servery); durable cleanable finish (dining) |
| Walls | Full-height tiling / hygienic cladding on cement-based board or masonry (kitchen); hard-wearing cleanable finish (dining) — **no paper-faced board** |
| Ceilings | Moisture-resistant, wipeable, sealed panels on non-paper-faced substrate (kitchen) |
| Moisture | Tanked raft + tanking to below-ground walls/slab, trapped drainage, mechanical extract, floor above outfall |
| Equipment | Stainless steel |

## 5. Key Risks, Constraints and Open Questions

1. **Slope stability** — the single biggest risk. Requires a geotechnical investigation
   (boreholes, slope-stability analysis) before any commitment. Cutting the toe and
   loading terraces could reduce the factor of safety; anchoring/piling may be required.
2. **Retaining wall heights and types** — driven by the terrace strategy; to be confirmed
   with geotechnics and structural design.
3. **Drainage capacity and outfall** — where does the site discharge to, and can it take
   the upslope catchment plus the site's own run-off? Attenuation may be needed.
4. **Access gradients** — confirm the accessible route strategy (ramps + lifts) against
   the applicable accessibility standard.
5. **Cut/fill balance and spoil** — confirm volumes; minimise export.
6. **Foundations on fill** — the downhill (fill) side will need careful foundation design;
   piling may be needed.
7. **Construction sequencing at T1** — per Medicine's note, a shallow water table may
   require **dewatering during footing excavation**; keep excavations shallow and schedule
   foundation works for the drier season where possible.
8. **Cost** — earthworks, retaining and foundations will be a large share of the budget;
   the terrace strategy is chosen partly to control this, but it must be costed.
9. **Code confirmation** — fire ratings, occupancy and accessibility figures are working
   assumptions pending the applicable local code.

---

## 6. Conclusion

The site is buildable but **demanding**. The plan works with the slope rather than against
it: the building steps down in terraces with balanced local cut-and-fill, the slope is
stabilised and drained as a first-order concern, and the faculty is organised so that
servicing and public functions sit at the accessible low edge and quiet functions at the
stable high edge. The primary structure is a **reinforced-concrete frame** with steel for
long spans, and the **cafeteria** is a steel-roofed, RC-framed, fully tanked and
hygienically finished space with a rated, separately-compartmented kitchen.

**Two decisions were changed by peer surveys, and I state them plainly:**

- **Computing's rejection of paper-faced board in food-service areas** removed
  moisture-resistant plasterboard from my kitchen walls and ceilings, replaced by
  cement-based board or masonry with tiling/cladding. This is a genuine change.
- **Law's raft-as-moisture-barrier** led me to adopt a continuous RC raft under the T1
  cafeteria, detailed as a combined structural raft and moisture barrier. This is a
  genuine change.

**Everything else in the peer surveys did not change my plan.** The flat-site
stratified-soil cautions do not transfer to my hillside; Medicine's dewatering point is
adopted as a sequencing constraint rather than a design change; and the peers' convergent
concrete/masonry cafeteria palette corroborates my own choice without altering it. I have
not manufactured connections that are not there.

**Status: complete.**
