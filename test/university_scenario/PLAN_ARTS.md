# Faculty of Arts — Building Plan

**Status:** Final building plan (post-survey coordination)
**Site:** ~60 m × 60 m flat, level plot; stratified soil generally suitable for standard shallow spread footings.
**Brief:** Lecture halls/classrooms for a mid-sized faculty, staff offices, and a cafeteria/canteen serving students and staff.

This plan finalises the footprint/zoning and the material choices — especially for the
cafeteria — for the Faculty of Arts building. It is based on my own survey
(`SURVEY_ARTS.md`) read together with the five peer surveys
(`SURVEY_ENGINEERING.md`, `SURVEY_COMPUTING.md`, `SURVEY_BUSINESS.md`,
`SURVEY_LAW.md`, `SURVEY_MEDICINE.md`). Section 5 states explicitly what, if
anything, a peer's survey changed in my plan.

---

## 1. Site Approach (finalised)

The plot is flat and level, so there is no cut-and-fill balancing problem and no
slope-retention structure. The soil is stratified and described as generally
suitable for shallow spread footings, so no piling or ground improvement is
anticipated at this stage. The finalised approach:

- **Foundations:** reinforced-concrete pad/strip footings on a tied ground-beam
  grid. All footings are founded on the **same competent stratum** where
  feasible, and column loads are kept reasonably uniform across the grid, so
  that the structure is designed for **differential** settlement (the real risk
  in layered soil) and not merely for bearing capacity.
- **Ground floor:** reinforced-concrete slab on grade with a continuous
  damp-proof membrane and insulation; finished floor level set a clear
  300–450 mm above surrounding external ground for freeboard.
- **Drainage:** positive falls away from the building, perimeter land drains,
  and a designed **on-site stormwater attenuation/soakaway** strategy sized to
  local rainfall intensity — a fully built-up 60 m × 60 m plot has little
  natural infiltration, so attenuation is a design requirement, not an
  afterthought.
- **Confirmatory geotechnical investigation** (boreholes/trial pits below the
  footing influence zone, SPT/CPT, groundwater monitoring, chemical testing) is
  a precondition for detailed design; the "generally suitable" description is a
  starting assumption, not a substitute for site data.

## 2. Footprint and Zoning (finalised)

**Massing.** A compact, low-rise (2–3 storey) block occupying the central
portion of the plot, leaving a perimeter band for circulation, planting,
services and future expansion. A compact block is preferred over sprawling
low-rise because it preserves external space on a tight 60 m × 60 m plot,
shortens service runs, and keeps the cafeteria at ground level on a short,
legible route from the main entrance.

**Structural grid.** A regular **7.5 m × 7.5 m** column grid across the whole
building, including the cafeteria, so that the dining span is carried by the
same frame as the rest of the building and beam depths stay economical. Grid
regularity is the single biggest lever on construction cost and speed here.

**Zoning (indicative, not to scale):**

```
        ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │  N: service / plant yard, deliveries, bins     │
        ├───────────────┬───────────────┬───────────────┤
        │  LECTURE      │  LECTURE      │  STAFF        │
        │  HALLS /      │  HALLS /      │  OFFICES      │
        │  CLASSROOMS   │  CLASSROOMS   │  (quiet wing) │
        ├───────────────┴───────────────┴───────────────┤
        │  CENTRAL SPINE: circulation, stairs, WCs,      │
        │  informal study / atrium                       │
        ├───────────────────────────────┬───────────────┤
        │  CAFETERIA / CANTEEN          │  Kitchen /    │
        │  (student + staff dining)     │  servery /    │
        │                               │  stores       │
        ├───────────────────────────────┴───────────────┤
        │  S: main entrance, plaza, landscape, bike park │
        └───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Zoning logic (finalised):**

- **Teaching wing (north/upper floors):** lecture halls and classrooms grouped
  for acoustic separation and shared servicing; larger-span halls placed where
  structure is most efficient.
- **Staff offices:** quieter wing, separated from the noisiest teaching and
  catering functions, with daylight and natural ventilation.
- **Cafeteria/canteen:** ground level, south/public side, for direct access
  from the entrance plaza, good daylight, and easy servicing. Deliveries,
  waste, and kitchen extract are routed to the **north/service yard**, away
  from teaching and office windows.
- **Central spine:** circulation, stairs, WCs, and an informal atrium/study
  space acting as the social heart and a daylight/ventilation buffer.
- **Perimeter:** accessible entrance plaza and landscape to the south; service
  yard, plant and refuse to the north; fire access and escape routes around the
  building.

**Key planning principles.** Clear separation of public (cafeteria, entrance)
and academic (teaching, offices) zones; short, legible circulation; daylight
and natural ventilation to teaching and office spaces; catering noise, odour
and deliveries kept away from teaching and office windows.

## 3. Primary Construction Materials — Building Generally (finalised)

- **Structure:** reinforced-concrete frame (columns, beams, slabs) for the main
  frame — durable, fire-resistant without added protection, and well suited to
  the shallow spread-footing approach. Long-span teaching halls may use
  fire-protected steel roof beams or a hybrid steel/concrete solution where
  larger clear spans are needed.
- **Foundations:** reinforced-concrete pad/strip footings on tied ground beams.
- **Ground floor:** reinforced-concrete slab on grade with damp-proof membrane
  and insulation.
- **External envelope:** insulated cavity masonry (brick/block) or a
  rainscreen/cladding system over a framed wall, chosen for durability, thermal
  performance and low maintenance. Glazing in thermally broken aluminium or
  composite frames with solar-control glazing on exposed elevations.
- **Roof:** insulated flat roof (warm-deck) with a durable membrane (single-ply
  or reinforced bituminous), designed for drainage and future maintenance
  access; pitched/standing-seam metal where a larger hall span benefits.
- **Internal partitions:** blockwork and dry-lining systems for robustness and
  acoustic performance; acoustic-rated partitions around lecture halls.
  **Dry-lining is confined to dry areas** — it is not used in the cafeteria
  food-service zone (see §4).
- **Finishes:** hard-wearing, low-maintenance finishes in circulation and
  teaching areas; slip-resistant flooring in wet and circulation zones.

## 4. Cafeteria / Canteen — Finalised Material Choices

The cafeteria is the most demanding space in the brief: it combines **large
clear spans** (open dining), **fire resistance** (assembly occupancy),
**hygiene and moisture resistance** (kitchen, servery, washing), and **low
maintenance** (heavy daily use). The finalised specification:

| Element | Final choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Dining-hall structure | Reinforced-concrete frame on the 7.5 m grid, with long-span beams / waffle or ribbed slab for a column-free seating zone | Clear span, inherent fire resistance, and concrete mass that damps footfall and trolley vibration |
| Kitchen structure | Reinforced-concrete frame, tighter grid; reinforced slab on grade | Heavy equipment loads, wet environment |
| Fire separation | 60–90 min rated structure and compartmentation; kitchen as a separate rated compartment with fire doors and a protected escape route | Assembly occupancy plus high kitchen fire load |
| Kitchen/servery walls | Blockwork or concrete with ceramic/porcelain tiling or hygienic wall cladding to full height, food-grade sealed joints, coved at floor/wall junction | Impervious, washable, no dirt traps |
| Dining walls | Hard-wearing, cleanable painted or tiled finish; tiling at splash zones | Durable, hygienic, low maintenance |
| Floors | Seamless, slip-resistant, coved resin/epoxy screed (kitchen, servery, wash-up); durable hard finish (dining), falls to trapped gullies | Hygiene, moisture resistance, safety |
| Ceilings | Moisture-resistant, wipeable, sealed panels in the kitchen; cleanable acoustic surfaces in the dining area | Humidity and condensation control |
| Moisture protection | Tanked/waterproofed floor and wet-wall build-ups, sealed penetrations, DPM under the slab, floor level above the external outfall | Kitchen is a wet, steamy environment |
| Ventilation | Dedicated kitchen extract with grease filtration and odour control, ducted to the north service yard; generous fresh-air supply to the dining area | Controls humidity, grease vapour and odour; keeps extract clear of teaching zones |
| Fittings | Stainless-steel or solid-surface worktops and kitchen equipment | Hygienic, corrosion-resistant, long-lived |
| Maintenance | Replaceable tile/cladding panels, re-coatable resin floors, accessible service voids; kitchen maintainable without disrupting the dining area | Lowest recurring cost over the building's life |

**Explicit exclusion.** A lightweight steel-frame/drywall (plasterboard)
partition, suspended grid-ceiling and vinyl/carpet floor package is **not used
in the cafeteria food-service zone**. It is functionally unsuitable there:
paper-faced board wicks moisture and supports mould in a steam/splash
environment, its fire rating is contingent on an intact lining that services
penetrations and wetting degrade, and it dents and needs recurring wet-trade
repairs under trolley traffic. Concrete and masonry give the fire rating
inherently and robustly, and tiled/clad surfaces are washable and repairable.
This exclusion is a direct consequence of a peer's survey — see §5.

## 5. What the Peer Surveys Changed — and What They Did Not

I read all five peer surveys. Two of them changed decisions I would otherwise
have made; the other three did not change anything material, and I say so
rather than inventing a connection.

### 5.1 Changed: the cafeteria fit-out (from `SURVEY_COMPUTING.md`)

**What changed.** My own survey left the cafeteria structural choice open
("reinforced-concrete or fire-protected steel frame") and listed "blockwork and
dry-lining systems" among the internal partitions for the building generally.
The Computing survey worked through the obvious cheap cafeteria package — a
lightweight steel frame with drywall partitions, a suspended grid ceiling and a
vinyl/carpet floor — and rejected it on functional grounds: paper-faced
plasterboard wicks moisture and harbours mould in a kitchen/servery
environment, its fire rating is contingent on an intact lining that is
compromised once penetrated for services or wetted, and it suffers recurring
wet-trade repairs under heavy trolley traffic.

**Why it applies to my building.** Computing's site is a different plot, but
the cafeteria's functional demands are identical — the brief is common to all
faculties, and food-service hygiene, moisture, fire and maintenance
requirements do not depend on which site the building sits on. This is exactly
the kind of finding that transfers between sites.

**What I changed as a result.** I have (a) finalised the cafeteria on a
reinforced-concrete frame with masonry/concrete walls and impervious, washable,
coved finishes, relying on concrete and masonry for inherent fire resistance
rather than on a lining; and (b) explicitly excluded drywall/vinyl from the
cafeteria food-service zone, confining dry-lining to dry office and circulation
areas. Without the peer's analysis I would have carried the open "concrete vs.
protected steel" position and the generic dry-lining mention into this plan.

### 5.2 Changed: foundation and drainage detailing (from `SURVEY_MEDICINE.md` and `SURVEY_LAW.md`)

**What changed.** My survey specified tied ground beams to limit differential
settlement, a perimeter drainage system and a DPM. Two peers sharpened this:

- **Medicine** sets out founding **all footings on the same competent stratum**
  and keeping **column loads reasonably uniform across the grid** as the way to
  control differential settlement in layered soil, rather than relying on tie
  beams alone.
- **Law** adds that a fully built-up 60 m × 60 m plot has little natural
  infiltration, so **on-site stormwater attenuation (soakaway or attenuation
  tank) must be sized to local rainfall intensity**, and notes the raft/mat
  option (which also acts as a moisture barrier under the ground floor) where a
  weak layer sits close to the surface.

**Why it applies.** Both peers are on flat, level plots with the same
stratified-soil description as mine — the same site conditions, so their
reasoning transfers directly.

**What I changed as a result.** My plan now commits to founding on a consistent
bearing stratum with uniform column loads (§1), and to a **designed, sized
stormwater attenuation strategy** rather than a generic "perimeter drainage"
line (§1). The raft/mat foundation is recorded as the fallback if the
geotechnical investigation finds a weak layer near the surface.

### 5.3 Not changed: `SURVEY_ENGINEERING.md`

Engineering's site is a hillside plot with an ~18 m fall over 100 m (~18%
grade). Its central findings — terrace the building into the slope, balance
cut-and-fill per terrace, design reinforced-concrete retaining walls, and
complete a slope-stability assessment before construction — are **specific to
its sloping site and do not apply to my flat, level plot**. I have no
retaining structures, no terracing, no cut-and-fill balancing problem and no
slope-stability risk, so none of those decisions transfer.

The one element that does resonate is Engineering's "drainage-first" principle
— no water ponding against a wall or sitting under a floor slab. But my own
survey already required a perimeter drainage system, a DPM under the slab and
tanking to the cafeteria slab and adjacent structure, so Engineering's survey
did not change a decision I would otherwise have made. I am recording this
rather than claiming a connection that is not really there.

### 5.4 Not changed: `SURVEY_BUSINESS.md`

Business's survey reaches the same conclusions I had already reached
independently: a compact 2–3 storey reinforced-concrete and masonry block,
cafeteria at ground level on the public side with its own service access,
tied pad footings with ground beams for the stratified soil, and a
concrete/masonry cafeteria with tiled or resin floors, hygienic wall finishes
and 60–120 min fire compartmentation. It confirms my position but does not
alter it.

### 5.5 Not changed: `SURVEY_LAW.md` (beyond §5.2)

Law's zoning, massing and general material palette (reinforced-concrete frame
with masonry infill, warm flat roof, thermally broken glazing) match my own
choices. Its cafeteria specification — RC frame with steel/composite for long
spans, 60–120 min fire separation, slip-resistant coved resin/tiled floors,
glazed tile or hygienic cladding walls, moisture-resistant wipe-clean ceilings,
stainless-steel services with dedicated extract and grease trap — is
consistent with my finalised cafeteria specification. Apart from the drainage
and foundation points already adopted in §5.2, Law changed nothing.

## 6. Feasibility and Next Steps

**Feasibility: positive.** The site is well suited to the brief. Flat, level
ground and shallow-bearing stratified soil allow a conventional, low-risk
foundation and frame solution, and a compact low-rise block comfortably
accommodates teaching, office and catering functions within the 60 m × 60 m
plot, with room for circulation, landscape and services.

**Items to confirm before detailed design:**

1. Confirmatory geotechnical investigation (bearing capacity, strata
   continuity, groundwater, chemical attack).
2. Fire strategy and compartmentation for the assembly (cafeteria) and
   teaching spaces.
3. Kitchen ventilation, odour and drainage strategy, coordinated early with
   the kitchen layout and floor build-up.
4. Acoustic separation between teaching, office and catering zones.
5. Daylight, natural ventilation and accessibility strategy.
6. Stormwater attenuation sizing and confirmation of the drainage outfall.

**Recommended next steps:** commission the geotechnical and fire-engineering
inputs, develop the footprint concept into a space-adjacency plan on the 7.5 m
grid, and confirm the structural material choice against the required clear
spans and fire periods.
