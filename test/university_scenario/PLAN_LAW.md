# Faculty of Law — Building Plan

**Status:** Final planning-phase building plan (post-survey coordination)
**Site:** Flat, level plot, approx. 60 m × 60 m (~3,600 m²)
**Soil:** Stratified (layered) profile, generally suitable for standard shallow
spread-footing foundations.
**Brief:** Lecture halls/classrooms for a mid-sized faculty, staff offices, and
a cafeteria/canteen serving students and staff.

This plan finalises the footprint/zoning concept and the material choices for
the Faculty of Law building, and records explicitly what (if anything) in the
five peer surveys changed a decision I would otherwise have made. My own
starting position is the Faculty of Law survey (`SURVEY_LAW.md`); the peer
surveys are `SURVEY_ENGINEERING.md`, `SURVEY_COMPUTING.md`,
`SURVEY_BUSINESS.md`, `SURVEY_MEDICINE.md` and `SURVEY_ARTS.md`.

---

## 1. Site Reading and Approach (unchanged in substance)

The plot is flat and level: no significant cut-and-fill, no retaining
structures, and drainage by gravity is possible with designed falls. The real
risks are **subsurface and hydrological**, driven by the stratified soil:

- **Differential settlement** across strata boundaries — footings founded on a
  consistent bearing stratum, tied with a continuous ground-beam/raft grid.
- **Water table and moisture** — confirm depth and seasonal fluctuation; DPM
  under the slab, DPC in walls, tanking where the water table is high.
- **Bearing capacity** — a confirmatory investigation to fix the allowable
  bearing pressure used in footing sizing.

**Founding strategy:** shallow reinforced-concrete pad/strip footings on a
competent stratum, with a **raft/mat** fallback where a weak layer sits close
to the surface (a raft also spreads load, tolerates modest differential
settlement, and acts as a moisture barrier under the ground floor).

## 2. Footprint and Zoning (finalised)

### 2.1 Massing

A single compact **2–3 storey block**, roughly rectangular, occupying the
central portion of the plot and leaving a perimeter band for circulation,
planting, services and stormwater attenuation. Indicative footprint
**~1,400–1,800 m²** (roughly 40–50% of the plot).

### 2.2 Zoning concept (finalised)

| Zone | Function | Notes |
|------|----------|-------|
| **A — Public/entry** | Entrance foyer, reception, admin, dean's office | Street-facing, clear wayfinding |
| **B — Teaching** | Lecture halls, seminar rooms, classrooms | Quiet side; stacked for acoustic separation |
| **C — Staff** | Offices, meeting rooms, staff room | Adjacent to teaching but acoustically buffered |
| **D — Social/cafeteria** | Canteen, servery, kitchen, seating | Ground floor, own entrance, near core |
| **E — Core/service** | Stairs, lifts, WCs, plant, storage | Central spine, repeated per floor |

### 2.3 Circulation, access and servicing

- Central circulation spine with a vertical core (stairs + lift) serving all
  floors; **two means of escape** for fire compliance.
- Separate **servicing access** for the kitchen (deliveries, waste) kept away
  from the main student entrance, with the **kitchen extract routed to the
  service yard** and away from teaching/office windows.
- Accessible (step-free) entrance and WCs throughout.
- Regular structural grid (target **~7.5–9 m bays**) with demountable
  partitions so classrooms can be reconfigured; generous floor-to-floor height
  for services and future adaptation.

## 3. Primary Construction Materials — Building Generally (finalised)

**Primary structural system: reinforced-concrete frame with masonry infill.**

- **Frame:** in-situ reinforced-concrete columns and beams (or flat slabs for
  flexibility and lower floor-to-floor height).
- **Floors:** reinforced-concrete slabs — good acoustic separation between
  stacked teaching floors and inherent fire resistance.
- **External walls:** clay/concrete block masonry with a ventilated cavity,
  insulation and a rainscreen or render finish.
- **Roof:** insulated flat roof (warm-deck) with a durable membrane
  (single-ply or built-up), designed for maintenance access and future PV.
- **Windows/glazing:** thermally broken aluminium or timber-aluminium frames,
  double/triple glazed, with solar control on exposed elevations.
- **Internal partitions:** lightweight drywall/blockwork, demountable where
  flexibility is needed.
- **Finishes:** durable, low-maintenance, hard-wearing.

**Alternative considered and not adopted as primary:** a steel frame with
composite deck floors would speed erection and give long spans, but offers less
inherent fire and acoustic mass and needs added fire protection — so concrete
is the primary system, with **steel used selectively where long spans are
needed** (large lecture halls, cafeteria dining area).

## 4. Cafeteria / Canteen — Finalised Material Choices

The cafeteria is the most demanding zone: **long clear spans**, **fire
separation**, **hygiene and moisture resistance**, and **low maintenance** in a
high-traffic, wet environment.

### 4.1 Structure and span

- **Long-span floor/roof over the seating area** to avoid columns in the dining
  space: a reinforced-concrete flat slab on a wider grid, or a
  **steel/composite beam** system where a longer, lighter span is advantageous.
  Target regular grid **~8–9 m**.
- Kitchen and servery use the standard concrete frame grid, with a
  **reinforced slab on grade** for the heavily loaded, wet kitchen zone.

### 4.2 Fire

- **Compartmentation:** fire-rated walls and floors (**60–120 min**, final
  rating confirmed against the applicable code) separating the kitchen from the
  dining area and the rest of the building, with fire-rated doors and dampers
  in ductwork.
- Concrete and masonry give inherent fire resistance; where steel is used,
  intumescent protection or board cladding achieves the required rating.
- **Kitchen suppression:** sprinkler coverage plus **kitchen hood suppression**
  over cooking equipment (adopted — see §5).
- **Kitchen extract ductwork: fire-rated and grease-tight**, with access for
  cleaning (adopted — see §5).

### 4.3 Hygiene and moisture resistance

- **Floors:** slip-resistant, seamless, coved **hygienic resin or tiled**
  finish with falls to trapped drainage; impervious and easy to clean.
- **Walls (kitchen/servery):** smooth, washable, impervious surfaces — glazed
  ceramic tiles or hygienic wall cladding to splashback height (full height in
  wet/kitchen zones), sealed joints.
- **Ceilings:** moisture-resistant, wipe-clean panels with access panels for
  services; no porous finishes.
- **Services:** stainless-steel kitchen equipment and worktops, grease traps,
  dedicated extract with filtration, easy-clean ductwork.
- **Vapour/moisture control:** robust ventilation and extraction to manage
  steam and condensation; sealed, moisture-resistant construction; tanked wet
  areas with floor falls to trapped gullies.

### 4.4 Maintenance

- Durable, replaceable, easy-clean materials: stainless steel, glazed tile,
  resin flooring, sealed concrete. Design for access to services (ceiling
  voids, service risers) so maintenance does not disrupt teaching.
- External: hard-wearing, easily cleaned finishes at ground level.

### 4.5 Summary of cafeteria material choices (final)

| Element | Choice | Why |
|---------|--------|-----|
| Structure | RC frame; steel/composite for long spans | Clear spans, fire mass |
| Fire separation | 60–120 min rated walls/floors, rated doors, rated grease-tight extract | Kitchen fire risk |
| Suppression | Sprinklers + kitchen hood suppression | Life safety |
| Floor | Slip-resistant seamless resin / tiled, coved | Hygiene, moisture, safety |
| Walls | Glazed tile / hygienic cladding | Washable, impervious |
| Ceiling | Moisture-resistant wipe-clean panels | Hygiene, condensation |
| Services | Stainless steel, dedicated extract + grease trap | Hygiene, fire, maintenance |

## 5. What the Peer Surveys Changed — and What They Did Not

I read all five peer surveys. Four of the six faculties (Computing, Business,
Law, Medicine, Arts) share the same flat, stratified-soil site type; Engineering
is on a steep hillside and is therefore the outlier. Below I separate the
findings that **genuinely changed** my plan from those that merely **confirmed**
it, and I state plainly where nothing changed.

### 5.1 Changes I have adopted

1. **Chemical testing of soil and groundwater (from Computing).**
   Computing's survey specifies sulphate/chloride and pH testing on soil and
   groundwater to select the correct **cement type and concrete cover for
   durability**. My own survey's geotechnical brief did not include this. I have
   **added it** to the investigation scope: on a stratified site with a
   potentially high water table, aggressive ground chemistry directly governs
   the concrete specification, and it is cheap to test and expensive to get
   wrong. *This is a change.*

2. **Finished-floor-level freeboard of 300–450 mm (from Computing).**
   My survey said only that finished floor levels should sit "above surrounding
   ground". Computing quantifies a **clear 300–450 mm freeboard** against
   surface water. I have **adopted the explicit figure** as the design target
   for the ground floor and the cafeteria. *This is a change.*

3. **Fire-rated, grease-tight kitchen extract ductwork with cleaning access
   (from Business).** My survey specified a dedicated extract with filtration
   and easy-clean ductwork, but did not state that the duct itself must be
   **fire-rated and grease-tight**. Business's survey makes this explicit, and
   it is a real fire-spread path in a kitchen. I have **adopted it** into the
   cafeteria fire strategy. *This is a change.*

4. **Kitchen hood suppression alongside sprinklers (from Engineering).**
   Engineering's cafeteria section assumes **sprinkler plus kitchen hood
   suppression**. My survey had compartmentation and rated construction but did
   not name suppression. I have **added sprinkler + hood suppression** to the
   cafeteria fire strategy. *This is a change.*

5. **Seismic/lateral detailing: continuous tied foundation and a regular
   structural layout to avoid torsional irregularity (from Medicine).**
   My survey did not address lateral/seismic considerations at all. Medicine's
   survey recommends a continuous, tied foundation and a regular layout even
   where seismicity is low. This is consistent with my raft/ground-beam
   approach and costs nothing extra to design in, so I have **adopted it as an
   explicit requirement**. *This is a change (an addition).*

6. **Dewatering and drier-season scheduling for footing excavation (from
   Medicine).** My survey flagged the water table as a risk but did not address
   construction-stage groundwater. Medicine's survey notes that with a shallow
   water table, **dewatering during footing excavation may be needed and
   foundation works should be scheduled for the drier season where possible**.
   I have **adopted this** into the construction approach. *This is a change.*

7. **Acoustic separation of catering from teaching/offices, and routing
   extract away from windows (from Arts).** My survey buffered lecture halls
   from the noisiest site edge but did not explicitly separate the cafeteria
   acoustically or route its extract away from teaching/office windows. Arts's
   survey makes both explicit. I have **adopted** the acoustic separation of
   the catering zone and the routing of kitchen extract/odour to the service
   yard, away from teaching and office windows. *This is a change.*

8. **Perimeter drainage channel and "no ponding" discipline (from
   Engineering).** Engineering's hillside survey is the most emphatic about
   drainage-first design and about never allowing water to pond against a wall
   or sit under a slab. Although Engineering's *terrain* is not mine, the
   principle transfers directly to my flat site (where water does not shed on
   its own). I have **adopted** a perimeter drainage channel and the explicit
   "no ponding against walls or under the slab" rule. *This is a change.*

### 5.2 Findings that confirmed my plan but did not change it

- **Computing's rejection of a lightweight drywall/vinyl cafeteria.** Computing
  tested the "office fit-out" package (steel frame, plasterboard partitions,
  grid ceiling, vinyl/carpet) against food-service constraints and rejected it
  on moisture/hygiene, fire and maintenance grounds. My survey had **already**
  chosen impervious, washable, coved finishes (resin/tile) on a concrete/masonry
  base, so this finding **confirms** my choice rather than changing it. I note
  it because it is the strongest independent corroboration of the cafeteria
  finish decision, and it reinforces the specific point that a fire rating
  contingent on an intact paper-faced lining is the wrong kind of rating — which
  is why my compartment boundaries are concrete/masonry, not board.
- **Steel for long spans over the dining hall (Engineering, Arts, Medicine).**
  Engineering proposes a steel portal/truss roof over the dining hall; Arts and
  Medicine allow steel/composite for the longest spans. My survey already
  allowed steel/composite for long spans, so this **confirms** rather than
  changes the position. The difference is one of emphasis, not decision.
- **RC frame as the primary structure (all five peers).** Every peer survey
  lands on a reinforced-concrete primary frame (Engineering with steel for long
  spans; the flat-site faculties with masonry infill). This **confirms** my
  primary structural choice.
- **Tiled/resin, coved, hygienic wet-area finishes (Computing, Business,
  Medicine, Arts).** All four flat-site peers specify essentially the same
  cafeteria wet-area palette I had chosen. **Confirmation, not change.**
- **60–120 min fire compartmentation for the kitchen (Business, Medicine,
  Law).** Consistent with my range. **Confirmation.**
- **Compact 2–3 storey massing on the 60 × 60 plot (Computing, Business,
  Medicine, Arts).** Consistent with my massing. **Confirmation.**

### 5.3 Where a peer's finding did NOT apply to me

- **Engineering's terrace-and-bridge strategy, retaining walls, slope
  stability, cut/fill balance and stepped access.** These are driven entirely by
  Engineering's 18 m fall over 100 m hillside site. My plot is flat, so none of
  the terrace, retaining-wall, slope-stability or stepped-access content
  transfers. I have deliberately **not** imported it. Only Engineering's
  *drainage-first* principle and its *cafeteria fire/suppression* detail
  transfer, and I have adopted those two items (see §5.1, items 4 and 8).
- **Engineering's "cafeteria at the lowest terrace, exposed to groundwater"
  tanking rationale.** My cafeteria is on flat ground, not at the toe of a
  slope, so the specific groundwater-exposure driver does not apply. I retain
  tanking to wet areas and where the water table is high, on my own site's
  merits, not because of Engineering's slope.

### 5.4 Bottom line

**Yes — peer surveys changed my plan.** Eight specific items were adopted
(§5.1): chemical durability testing; a quantified 300–450 mm FFL freeboard;
fire-rated grease-tight kitchen extract; kitchen hood suppression; explicit
seismic/lateral tied-foundation detailing; dewatering/drier-season scheduling;
acoustic separation of catering with extract routed away from windows; and a
perimeter drainage channel with a no-ponding rule. The most consequential were
the **durability/chemical testing** and the **fire-rated grease-tight extract
plus hood suppression**, because both close real gaps in my original cafeteria
and foundation specifications.

Everything else in the peer surveys either **confirmed** choices I had already
made (RC frame, impervious coved cafeteria finishes, 60–120 min
compartmentation, compact massing, steel for long spans) or was **specific to
Engineering's hillside site** and correctly does not transfer to my flat plot.
I have not manufactured connections that are not there.

## 6. Feasibility and Next Steps

**Feasibility: positive.** The flat site and generally suitable stratified soil
support a conventional shallow-foundation, concrete-frame building. The main
technical attention points are (a) confirming stratification, water table and
ground chemistry by geotechnical investigation, (b) designing for differential
settlement (raft/stiffened foundation), and (c) robust moisture, drainage and
compartmentation detailing — especially for the cafeteria.

**Recommended next steps**

1. Commission geotechnical and topographical surveys, **including
   sulphate/chloride/pH chemical testing** and groundwater monitoring.
2. Confirm local planning, fire and accessibility requirements, including the
   final fire-resistance period for the cafeteria compartment.
3. Develop the zoning concept into a schematic layout with a structural grid.
4. Size stormwater attenuation and confirm service connections and outfall.
5. Cost-plan the concrete-frame option against a steel alternative for the
   long-span dining area.
