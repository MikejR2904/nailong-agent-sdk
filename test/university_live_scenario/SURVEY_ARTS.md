# Faculty of Arts — Initial Site / Feasibility Survey

Stage: university-survey (early feasibility). Site facts are given, not chosen:
flat, level ground, ~60 m × 60 m (≈3,600 m²); stratified (layered) soil,
generally suitable for standard shallow spread-footing foundations.

This is an independent survey. The other five faculties are surveying their own
sites concurrently; I have not seen their documents. Peer findings that arrived
live during this work are addressed in the final section.

---

## 1. Approach to terrain and site conditions

**Flat, level ground.** The absence of slope is a benefit for accessibility,
drainage strategy and construction logistics, but it removes gravity drainage as
a free option. The site will be drained by a designed system, not by fall:

- Finished floor levels set a minimum of 150 mm above surrounding external
  levels, with a perimeter apron and a shallow fall away from the building.
- A perimeter land-drain / French drain plus a positive piped storm system to
  the site outfall; the flat site means the outfall invert, not the building,
  governs the drainage design. Confirm outfall capacity and invert early — this
  is a feasibility-level risk, not a detail.
- Attenuation (below-ground crate storage or a shallow basin at one corner) is
  likely to be required because a flat site cannot shed water quickly.
- All external circulation to be step-free (max 1:20 ramps where levels change)
  to serve the faculty's accessibility obligations.

**Stratified soil.** "Suitable for standard shallow spread footings" is a
statement about *bearing capacity*, not about *uniformity*. On a layered
profile, capacity and stiffness vary with the depth and thickness of each
stratum, so a single large footprint can straddle a stratum transition and
settle differentially even though every individual footing passes its own check.
My approach:

1. Commission a borehole / test-pit grid across the **whole** footprint (not one
   central hole) to establish stratum continuity, depth and thickness before the
   foundation design is fixed.
2. Keep column bases on the same stratum where possible; where a transition
   crosses the footprint, step or deepen footings across it.
3. Where a large-span or heavily loaded area cannot avoid the transition, use a
   stiffened strip or a raft to redistribute settlement.
4. Design ground-floor slabs and drainage to tolerate small differential
   movement: movement joints, flexible service connections, and slab
   reinforcement detailed for it.

This caution was raised as a live finding to the other faculties (see §5); it is
recorded here because it applies to my own site too.

**Other site conditions to verify at feasibility:** groundwater level and
seasonal variation (affects excavation and any raft), sulphate/aggressive ground
chemistry (affects concrete mix and buried services), and any made ground or
buried obstructions from previous use.

## 2. Zoning and footprint concept

Brief: lecture halls/classrooms for a mid-sized faculty, staff offices, and a
cafeteria/canteen serving students and staff.

**Concept: three-zone "L" with a central spine, cafeteria as a distinct,
fire-separated block.**

- **Zone A — Teaching (north/west, the larger area).** Lecture halls and
  classrooms, the quietest and most acoustically sensitive functions, placed
  away from the site entrance and from the kitchen's service yard. Two large
  flat-floor lecture halls (approx. 200 and 120 seats) plus a cluster of
  general teaching rooms. Large spans here drive the structural grid (see §3).
- **Zone B — Staff offices (east edge).** Cellular and small-group offices along
  a naturally lit edge, with a separate, quieter entrance and direct access to
  the teaching zone. Office floor-to-floor is lower than the teaching zone; the
  step in height is used to break the massing and to place plant.
- **Zone C — Cafeteria/canteen (south, at the main student route).** Dining hall
  plus kitchen, servery and stores, positioned for public/student access without
  crossing the teaching zone, with its own service yard, delivery access and bin
  store on the site boundary. The kitchen is treated as a **separate
  fire-compartment** (see §4).
- **Spine.** A single circulation spine links A, B and C, doubling as the
  accessible route and the primary escape route. It is the one element that
  crosses the whole site, so it is kept on a simple, regular grid and its
  foundations are the first place the stratified-soil caution is applied.

**Indicative areas (feasibility order-of-magnitude, to be tested):**

| Element | Indicative area |
|---|---|
| Lecture halls / classrooms | ~1,100 m² |
| Staff offices + support | ~600 m² |
| Cafeteria: dining + servery | ~450 m² |
| Kitchen, stores, back-of-house | ~250 m² |
| Circulation, WCs, plant, storage | ~500 m² |
| **Total gross** | **~2,900 m²** |

At ~2,900 m² gross on a 3,600 m² plot, the building is a **two-storey** solution
with a footprint of roughly 1,500–1,700 m², leaving the balance for the entrance
square, service yard, cycle parking and landscaping. A single-storey scheme
would consume nearly the whole plot and leave no external amenity, so two
storeys is the working assumption. The cafeteria is kept at ground level for
deliveries, drainage falls and student access; teaching sits above and beside it.

**Grid.** A regular structural grid (indicatively 7.5 m × 7.5 m for teaching and
office bays, widened locally for the large lecture halls) keeps foundations
repetitive — which is exactly what the layered-soil condition rewards — and lets
the cafeteria's different servicing needs be accommodated as a local variation
rather than a site-wide complication.

## 3. Primary construction materials — building generally

**Structure: reinforced concrete frame with steel long-span roof over the
lecture halls.**

- *Reinforced concrete* for columns, cores and the general floor plates. Reasons:
  it is robust and low-maintenance, gives good acoustic mass between stacked
  teaching floors (a real requirement for a faculty with lecture halls above
  other rooms), provides inherent fire resistance without added protection, and
  performs well on a site where differential settlement is a live risk — a
  stiff, monolithic frame tolerates and redistributes small movements better
  than a light, articulated one.
- *Structural steel* for the long-span roofs over the large lecture halls, where
  a column-free span is functionally necessary. Steel is protected for fire
  (intumescent coating or board) and the roof build-up is detailed for acoustic
  separation from the floor below.
- *Foundations:* shallow reinforced-concrete pad/strip footings, sized and
  stepped per the borehole grid in §1. A raft is held in reserve for the spine
  and any bay that cannot avoid a stratum transition.

**Envelope: brick/block masonry cavity wall with a ventilated rainscreen or
render, and aluminium-framed double-glazed windows.**

- Masonry is durable, low-maintenance, has good thermal mass and acoustic
  performance, and is a familiar, repairable construction for a public
  institution. It also gives the fire separation between zones for free.
- Insulated cavity with a drained and ventilated outer leaf; windows in
  thermally broken aluminium with double glazing and solar control to the
  south/west teaching elevations.
- Roof: warm flat roof (insulated, single-ply membrane) over the general
  building, with a pitched or standing-seam metal roof over the large halls if
  the span and drainage strategy favour it.

**Internal finishes (general teaching/office):** painted plasterboard or
plaster on masonry, suspended acoustic ceilings in teaching rooms, carpet or
vinyl in offices, hard-wearing vinyl or sealed screed in circulation. These are
the *dry* finishes — and they are deliberately **not** carried into the kitchen
(see §4).

**Services:** natural ventilation with openable windows and stack effect for
teaching and offices (low energy, low maintenance, appropriate for a temperate
site), with mechanical ventilation only where required — the kitchen, WCs and
internal rooms. Heating via a low-temperature water system with radiators or
underfloor in the dining hall.

## 4. Cafeteria/canteen — specific materials and construction

The cafeteria is the most demanding part of the brief: it combines a long-span
public dining space with a commercial kitchen that is simultaneously a **fire
hazard**, a **wet/hygiene zone**, and a **high-maintenance** area. The four
criteria named in the brief — structural span, fire rating, hygiene/moisture
resistance, maintenance — are addressed in turn.

**Structural span (dining hall).** The dining hall should be column-free or
near-column-free to allow flexible seating and servery layouts. Indicatively a
12–15 m clear span, achieved with a steel roof structure (beams or a shallow
truss) on concrete columns, or a post-tensioned concrete slab if the floor above
requires it. The kitchen, by contrast, is a cellular, heavily serviced space on a
tight grid — the two are structurally different and are deliberately separated
by a movement/fire joint so that the long-span dining volume and the stiff
kitchen box do not fight each other.

**Fire rating.** The kitchen is a high fire-hazard compartment and is separated
from dining, teaching and office areas by a **60-minute fire-rated wall with
self-closing fire doors** (a common and defensible standard for a commercial
kitchen; confirm against the local code and the fire strategy). The dining hall
is a separate compartment from the teaching zone. Escape routes from the dining
hall are direct to outside, not through the kitchen.

**Hygiene and moisture resistance.** Kitchen and dishwash zones get
non-combustible, non-porous, washable surfaces throughout:

- Walls: stainless-steel or FRP (fibre-reinforced plastic) panels, or
  cement/glass-mat board — **not** paper-faced gypsum board, which grease vapour
  and sustained moisture destroy and which cannot be washed.
- Ceilings: a washable, non-porous, non-combustible system — **not** a standard
  suspended mineral-fibre ceiling, which is unsuitable in a commercial kitchen.
- Floors: coved, slip-resistant, tanked wet-area finish with falls to drains and
  a grease trap/interceptor on the drainage line. The kitchen floor build-up is
  deliberately different from the dry teaching floors.
- Dining hall: hard-wearing, easily cleaned floor (sealed screed or heavy-duty
  vinyl/tile), washable wall finishes to splash height, and a ceiling that can be
  cleaned.

**Kitchen exhaust and suppression.** Grease-laden cooking vapour is a recognised
fire hazard: grease accumulates on duct walls, can ignite from a cookline
flare-up, and fire can travel inside the duct through otherwise fire-separated
compartments. Therefore:

- A dedicated, continuously welded or flanged **liquid-tight grease duct** (not
  ordinary galvanised spiral duct), with access doors at every change of
  direction for cleaning.
- The duct enclosed in a fire-rated shaft, or given a listed fire-rated wrap,
  where it passes through or adjacent to other occupancies; terminated well clear
  of air intakes, windows and combustible roof build-up.
- A dedicated **kitchen suppression system** (hood + duct), interlocked to shut
  down the supply fan and fuel on activation.
- A grease trap/interceptor on the drainage line.

**Maintenance.** All kitchen surfaces are chosen to be cleanable and repairable
by the institution's own maintenance team: stainless and FRP panels are
replaceable in sections; the grease duct has cleaning access by design; the
dining floor is a single, repairable finish. The kitchen is placed on the ground
floor with direct service-yard access so that deliveries, waste and duct
cleaning do not pass through teaching or office space.

**Zoning consequence.** These decisions are not finishes-level details — they
drive the ceiling void depth (grease duct and its fire enclosure), the roof
penetrations and shaft locations, the drainage falls and the compartmentation of
the cafeteria block. They are therefore fixed at feasibility, in the zoning
layout, not deferred.

## 5. Influence of peer findings (live, from open_questions)

Three findings were visible in `open_questions` while this survey was written.
I assessed each independently.

1. **[Faculty of Computing] Grease-laden kitchen exhaust ducts.** *Applies to my
   site — adopted.* Every faculty brief includes a cafeteria, and this is a
   functional fire hazard, not a site-specific one. It changed my survey: §4 now
   specifies a dedicated liquid-tight grease duct with cleaning access doors, a
   fire-rated shaft or wrap, a hood+duct suppression system interlocked to the
   supply fan and fuel, a grease trap, and a 60-minute separation between kitchen
   and dining/teaching. It also changed the **zoning**: the duct's fire enclosure
   and cleaning access drive ceiling void depth and shaft locations, so the
   cafeteria block is laid out with a dedicated vertical shaft and roof
   penetration from the outset.

2. **[Faculty of Business] Kitchen/dishwash finishes and compartmentation.**
   *Applies to my site — adopted, and it reinforced the above.* It confirmed that
   standard lightweight drywall/gypsum partitions and suspended mineral-fibre
   ceilings are unsuitable in kitchen and dishwash zones. My §4 therefore
   specifies non-combustible, non-porous, washable wall and ceiling surfaces
   (stainless/FRP, cement/glass-mat board), a 60-minute kitchen compartment, and
   coved, slip-resistant, tanked wet-area floors with falls to drains — distinct
   from the dry teaching floor build-up. This is consistent with, and partly
   overlaps, finding 1; I have kept both because they address different aspects
   (exhaust/suppression vs. finishes/compartmentation).

3. **[Faculty of Arts — my own finding] Stratified-soil geotechnical caution.**
   Raised by me and broadcast live. It is recorded in §1 and applied to my own
   design: a borehole/test-pit grid across the whole footprint, column bases kept
   on the same stratum or stepped across transitions, a stiffened strip or raft
   held in reserve for the spine and any bay crossing a transition, and movement
   joints and flexible service connections in the ground-floor slabs and
   drainage.

No peer finding was rejected as inapplicable to this site. Both peer findings
concern the cafeteria, which every faculty shares, so both were directly
relevant; neither required a change of site strategy, only of cafeteria
construction and compartmentation, which is where they were applied.

## 6. Feasibility risks and next steps

- **Differential settlement on layered soil** — the principal geotechnical risk;
  resolved by the borehole grid and foundation strategy in §1. Do not let a
  single central borehole stand in for a footprint-wide investigation.
- **Flat-site drainage** — outfall invert and attenuation capacity to be
  confirmed before layout is fixed.
- **Kitchen fire strategy** — compartmentation, duct enclosure and suppression
  to be developed with the fire engineer; they constrain the zoning, not just the
  finishes.
- **Two-storey assumption** — to be tested against the accommodation schedule;
  if the brief grows, the plot is the binding constraint, not the structure.
- **Next steps:** footprint-wide ground investigation; drainage/outfall survey;
  fire strategy workshop; accommodation schedule to confirm areas and storey
  count.
